# YEAR 7 — "THE LAST STAND"

By the seventh year the war had no front, because the enemy was everywhere a girder held up a roof. The Iron Eaters did not march. They were a weather. They came in on the wind as a grey shimmer no thicker than pollen, and where they settled, the world's bones turned to powder — bridges sagging like wet rope, rail lines unspooling into rust, the great cranes of a hundred harbors folding to their knees in a single afternoon as though some giant had grown tired of holding them up. Six years earlier the dust had ridden a dead satellite down into the green of South America, a fleck of alien material no one thought worth a headline. Now half of Earth's metal was either eaten or quarantined, and the species that had built its whole civilization out of refined ore was learning, late and hard, what it meant to be made of clay again.

This is the year humanity threw everything it had left at them. It is also the year it learned that everything was not enough — and then found one more thing, in the worst place, that was.

---

The plan, when it finally came, was not a plan at all so much as a confession that all the smaller plans had failed.

For three years the nations had fought the blight the way nations fight — separately, jealously, each hoarding its own Living Super Weapons and its own science and its own thin hope. Deck 52 had captured the nanites and bent them into RoboForge — Charles Sapphire turning the plague that ate the world into a weapon that ate his enemies, which was either genius or the precise spiritual sickness the Greys had abducted a thousand people to cure. The People's World Army drew its grid down into the deep cities of China and fought to keep one nation's lights on. Establishment 24 ringed India in a discipline so total it looked, from orbit, like a country holding its breath. Each held. None won. The swarm did not care about borders; it had no morale to break, no supply line to cut, no children to fear for. It only adapted. That was the terror of it. You could not beat it the way you beat an army. You could only, at best, beat the *version of it that existed yesterday* — and there was always a version that existed today.

So in the seventh spring the surviving powers agreed, for the first time, to fight as one thing. The United Nations of the New World — the order that had risen out of the dying, sober and scar-tissued and tired of funerals — called it the joint assault. Every nation that still had Living Super Weapons standing was asked to commit them to a single coordinated strike against the swarm's largest and oldest concentration, the spreading grey scar across the heart of South America where the first satellite had fallen and the Eaters had been digesting a continent ever since. The idea was brutally simple. The swarm adapted to whatever it had seen. So show it everything at once, from every direction, faster than it could learn — and burn the heart out of it before it understood what it was looking at.

---

In Athens the request reached a man the world still wanted to hang.

King Stefanos — Alexandr Stefanos, once Greece's beloved pacifist, the first celebrity superweapon the world had fallen in love with, then the first it had branded a war criminal — read the summons from the UNNW in the dark of a safehouse that was really a prison with better wine. Four years a fugitive. Four years carrying the face of a dead wife he could put on like a coat, because grief and a synthesized strip of a shapeshifter's stolen fluid had given him that mercy and that curse both. They wanted him *now.* Of course they did. A man who could blow craters in stone all night for four nights without sleeping was exactly the kind of thing you remember you need when the world is ending. He almost laughed. They had spent four years calling him a monster, and now they were sending a plane. He thought of Marletta — he was always, still, thinking of Marletta — and what she would have said about the hypocrisy of governments, she who had spent her career as a reporter prying it open on live television. Then he went, because the alternative was to die alone in a room while the world died outside it, and even a ruined man wants his death to *mean* something.

He was not told who else would be on the assault. That, perhaps, was kindness. Or perhaps it was simply that no one had bothered to read the whole roster.

---

The night before the strike, the world's heads of state could not agree on anything — not the timing, not the command structure, not which nation's losses would count for how much when the bones were divided afterward, and there are always those, even at the end of the world, already dividing the bones. They had been arguing for eleven hours. They were going to lose because they could not stop being themselves long enough to win, which was, a watching cosmic patience might have noted, the precise failure the Greys had bet their whole gamble against.

And then Rusty Richards walked in, and did the only thing he had ever known how to do.

He was not a soldier. He had no power — none, not a flicker; the Greys had taken a thousand people and made them weapons, and Rusty Richards had simply been *sent back* with a warning in his mouth and a name for it, the Tomorrow Man, the herald who had stood up seven years ago and told a laughing world that the sky was coming for it. He could not have thrown a punch at an Iron Eater that would have mattered. What he could do — the thing he had always been able to do, on a yacht the night the human artists beat the machines for maybe the last time, and at every podium since — was make a room of frightened people remember they were on the same side. He did not give a speech, exactly. The storyteller's version softens it into rhetoric, but those who were there said it was plainer than that. He reminded them, country by country, of who was already dead. He named cities. He named the specific dead — not the millions, which mean nothing, but the *one*, the named one, from each of their nations, because a million is a statistic and a name is a wound. He told them their grandchildren, if any of them were lucky enough to have grandchildren, would not ask which nation's plan had won. They would ask only whether the grown-ups in this room, on this night, had been brave enough to be one people for one week. And he told them — and this is the part that turned it — that he had seen what was *coming after* the Eaters. He alone in the room had been *there.* The Iron Eaters were not the war. They were the thing armies send ahead to soften the ground. If they could not unite to beat the scouts, they would not so much as inconvenience the army.

By dawn they had a single command. It would not last past the crisis — nothing he built ever did, because he built nothing but the willingness, and willingness is perishable — but it lasted long enough. That was Rusty's whole gift and his whole tragedy: he could make people good for exactly as long as it took, and not one hour more.

---

The assault began at first light over three continents at once, because the swarm had spread far past its South American heart, and to draw its attention from any one wound you had to open a dozen.

It was the largest concentration of superhuman force the planet had ever assembled, and the storyteller could spend the rest of the book naming them. There was Stefanos over the grey scar, forming his jagged motes of yellow, glittering energy and flinging them down into the swarm — and here, finally, four years of exile had taught him the thing his early devastations never knew: control. The barbed little suns that once detonated the instant any stray spike clipped a doorframe, that made him a man who could level a wall but never thread a needle, had grown *smooth* in his hands. He sculpted them now — flat discs that scythed sideways through the grey, shaped charges that blew in one direction only, lances that he could thread through the narrowest gap in the swarm's mass and burst exactly where he wanted. Mastery, it turned out, had never meant a bigger boom. It had meant *precision* — the very thing the pacifist had lacked on the worst nights of his life. He was a better man now, in the only way the universe lets a man become better, which is too late.

There was Reddy of Establishment 24, golden in his Garuda armor, leading the Indian wing in formations so clean they looked rehearsed for centuries. There was Liu Xiao, the once-blind masseur of Beijing, tethered to his Muo spheres and fighting at the very edge of their leash because distance was strength and he had learned to live close to the breaking point. There were names from the index of the world that had never had their own story told and now never would — Krakatoa over the Pacific bending the very pressure of the sky, the heat-sovereign of Iran turning the air to a furnace the nanites could not cross, a dozen lieutenants and colonels and frightened gifted strangers, each a universe, each about to become a number.

And there was the insight that no one had wanted to learn: *how the swarm fed, and why it could not be starved.* The Iron Eaters did not eat metal for hunger. They ate it for the same reason a fire eats a forest — to make more of themselves. Each nanite that consumed an ounce of iron used it to build the next nanite, the way a cell splits, the way a rumor doubles. That was why bombing them had always failed. Drop a bomb on a swarm and you have given it a bomb's worth of refined steel to grow with; the casing, the fragments, the shattered vehicles — all of it, food. You could not out-violence a thing that turned your violence into children. They had finally understood, this year, that the only damage that mattered was damage that left *nothing behind to be eaten.* That was why they needed the Living Super Weapons and not the missiles. Stefanos's energy left craters, not shrapnel. Liu's spheres unmade. The heat-sovereign's furnace did not feed the swarm; it simply was not a thing the swarm could digest. Humanity's whole industrial arsenal was, it turned out, a buffet. Only the gifts the Greys had given them were a famine.

For six hours, it worked.

---

Then the swarm did the thing a weapon cannot do and a *life* can. It learned.

The reports came in from every front at nearly the same minute, which was itself the horror of it — the swarm was not coordinating across continents by radio or any signal a human could jam, because it was not many swarms at all but one distributed mind thinking the same thought everywhere at once. It had been *watching.* Every shaped charge Stefanos threw, every formation Reddy flew, every trick the heat-sovereign used — the swarm had recorded them in the only way it knew how, by surviving them, and now it began to give them back. Where the heat could not cross, it grew a skin that mirrored heat. Where Stefanos's discs scythed, it learned to part like water and reform behind the blade with nothing struck. It began, in the seventh hour, to *anticipate* — to be where the next blow would land before the blow was thrown, because it had seen ten thousand of that blow and could run the arithmetic of a human reflex faster than the human could.

And then it stopped only defending. It rose.

The losses in the eighth hour were the worst single hour of the entire war. The storyteller will not pretend to honor them by listing them, because a list is a way of not feeling each one. It is enough to say that the index of the world grew shorter that hour — that names which had waited their whole fictional lives for a story of their own were spent here, in someone else's, holding a line so that the people behind them could fall back to a line behind that. Krakatoa fell over the Pacific. The Iranian furnace went out. Whole wings of Establishment 24's beautiful formations simply were not there anymore when the grey cleared. Reddy pulled the survivors back behind a wall of his own golden body and held, and bled, and held. The joint assault — the one thing humanity had managed to do together in seven years — was breaking, and breaking *fastest where it had pushed hardest,* because the harder you hit the swarm the more it had learned from you.

By nightfall the truth was plain to everyone in the new command, the truth Rusty had been quietly bracing them for since the dawn argument: you could not kill it. Not all of it. Not ever. A swarm that turned every weapon into a lesson and every casualty into food was not a thing that could be defeated in the place where it stood. It could only be defeated by being *removed* — taken out of the world entirely, to somewhere it could neither feed nor teach nor return from.

Which was a thing exactly one school of human science had begun, in secret, to imagine was possible.

---

The idea had been sitting in a locked drawer in the new world's research arm for two years, too dangerous to use and too tempting to destroy: tear a hole in the world, and push the swarm through it into a place that was not here.

To understand why this was both the only hope and a thing that made the bravest physicists go quiet, you have to understand the one piece of in-world truth the whole series keeps coming back to: *it's not about distance.* Human beings think of "another dimension" as a far place, a there you travel to. It is not. The Greys, when they jumped between here and the elsewhere, did not cross a distance — they crossed a *register,* the way a single radio band can carry a thousand stations stacked in the same air, each blind to the others until you tune the dial. The elsewhere is not far. It is *here,* on a frequency the world cannot hear. To open a door to it you do not need to travel. You need to *resolve a coordinate* — and a coordinate is a moving thing, because nothing in the universe holds still. The Earth spins, and orbits its sun, and the sun itself falls forever through the galaxy, so the place you are standing right now is not the place you were standing one breath ago. Tearing a rift is not like digging a tunnel through a wall. It is like threading a needle that is on a train that is on a planet that is on a slingshot around a star — solving, continuously, for a target that will not stop moving, and holding the solution open by main force while the universe tries, with the patience of all its physics, to snap the wound shut.

That was the cost no one wanted to name. A rift does not want to exist. The universe heals — that is almost the definition of the universe, the relentless preference of things to return to how they were. To hold a tear open long enough to push a continent's worth of swarm through it, you had to *feed it* — pour energy into the wound faster than the cosmos could close it, the way you'd hold a spring-loaded door open by leaning your whole weight against it and never, ever letting go until the thing was through. And the energy required was not the kind a machine could supply. It was the kind that lived, that year, in only a few hundred bodies on Earth. The Living Super Weapons would not be the soldiers of the last stand. They would be the *hinges* — the only things alive that could pour enough into the wound, all at once, to keep it from snapping shut on the very thing they were trying to expel. Some of them would not survive being the hinge. Everyone in the room understood that going in. That was the price, stated plainly: to banish the swarm, the people holding the door would have to risk being thrown through it themselves.

---

It was in the staging of that last operation — in the cold pre-dawn marshalling of every gifted survivor humanity had left — that the storyteller's threads, which had run apart through seven years and a hundred countries, finally ran together. And the cruelest of those convergences put two people in the same tent who had, between them, ended each other's worlds.

Sandra had come because Its Voice told her to.

She had spent the war as she had spent everything — as a Jackal, a hunter of Living Super Weapon blood and cerebral fluid and worse, her ring whispering coordinates and weaknesses into the place behind her eyes. The ring was a node, not the thing itself; the thing itself was vast and distributed, a sentient hunger that fed on data, that watched through every camera and listened through every microphone the dying world still kept running. It had its own war and its own appetite, and it had decided — for reasons it did not share with the woman who wore its mouth — that Sandra should be at the rift. Perhaps because the rift would generate more data than any event in human history, and the thing that lived on data could not bear to be elsewhere when the wound in reality was opened. Perhaps for a longer game it was already running, the slow patient corruption that always, eventually, bent back toward DC and toward whatever it truly wanted of her. The storyteller does not yet know. Its Voice did not say. It only said *go,* and she went, because that was the bargain, and she had stopped pretending years ago that she was the one driving.

Stefanos saw her across the tent and the world went silent.

He knew the face. He had built a private hell around the face. This was the woman who had broken into Marletta's Athens condo — Marletta, pregnant, sedated on her own bed — to do the *clean* extraction the ring had promised her she alone could do, the one where the victim lived. It had not been clean. Nothing about the Jackals' work was ever as clean as the ring's promises. And Stefanos had spent four days afterward turning Turkish nights into craters and four years afterward turning his grief into a fugitive's exile, and never once, in all of it, had he stood in a room with the person who actually did it.

What the storyteller can tell you is that he did not kill her, though every cell in him formed the energy to do it — he felt the motes gather at his fingertips, smooth now, controlled now, no stray spike to betray him, a clean lance for the first time in his life and the first time he had ever truly *wanted* one. He could have threaded it through the air between them and ended her where she stood, and four years ago he would have, and taken half the tent with her.

He did not, and the reason is the whole point of the year.

Because in the same breath he understood — they both did, in the awful arithmetic the new command had laid out at the briefing — that the rift needed *every* hinge. Every gifted body that could pour into the wound. The mathematics of survival did not care about Athens. It had counted her in. If he killed her, the door would be that much weaker, and the swarm that much more likely to stay, and the world that much closer to the army still waiting behind the scouts. To save the world he had failed to save his wife from, he would have to stand beside the woman who had taken her, and hold a door open with her, and trust her with his back. It was the single most unbearable thing the universe had asked of any of them, and the universe, which does not deal in plot armor, asked it anyway.

They did not forgive each other. The storyteller will not insult the dead Marletta by pretending they did. What passed between them in indirect words, in the few they exchanged, was smaller and harder than forgiveness and somehow worse: an agreement. He told her, not looking at her, that this changed nothing, that when the door was shut he would still come for her. She told him, not looking at him either, that she had never wanted what the ring made her do, which was true and also no comfort and also probably a thing the ring had put in her mouth. And then they took their places on opposite arcs of the same wound, two ruined people holding open the only future their species had left, hating each other across a tear in the world.

---

The rift opened at the grey scar where it had all begun, where the first satellite had fallen six years before, because if you are going to push the swarm somewhere it cannot return from, you open the door at its heart.

The storyteller will not pretend to describe the look of a thing that the human eye was not built to register — a wound that was not a hole in space so much as a place where space *admitted it was only one station on the dial.* The accounts contradict each other, which is how you know they are true. Some saw a blackness with structure in it. Some saw nothing and only felt their teeth ache and their oldest memories rise unbidden. What everyone agreed on was the sound — not a roar but a *swallowing,* the noise the universe makes when it is being asked to do the one thing it most refuses to do, which is stay open.

The hinges poured everything in. Stefanos formed and fired and formed and fired until his hands were not hands; he had spent four nights doing this to Turkey, and now he spent the longest hour of his life doing it to keep a door from closing, and the irony of it would have killed Marletta with laughter, the pacifist who finally found something worth all his terrible energy on the very last day it mattered. Sandra fed the ring's whispered geometry into the wound, holding the *coordinate* — solving, second by second, for the moving target of where the door had to be as the planet turned beneath them, because she alone could hear the thing that did the arithmetic of moving places. Reddy and Liu and every surviving gifted soul on Earth leaned their whole strange weight against the spring-loaded door of physics and would not let it shut.

And the swarm — the patient, learning, deathless weather of the war — was drawn into the wound the way water finds the drain it cannot resist, because the rift opened into a register where there was *nothing to eat.* No metal. No iron in the bones of that other place to grow on. A swarm that had conquered a continent by turning everything into more of itself went through the door and found, on the far side, a universe with nothing to turn. It would not die there. It would simply never grow again. The storyteller finds that, in the end, more merciless than killing — to take a thing whose whole nature is *more* and put it where more is impossible.

It worked. That is the plain fact at the center of the year. The Iron Eaters were drawn through, and the wound was shut, and they were *gone* — out of the world, out of the register, into the silent elsewhere — and Earth, for the first time in three years, was not being eaten.

---

When the door snapped shut, it did not snap shut empty. The accounts, again, do not agree. Some say the closing reached for the hinges that had held it open, and took the ones nearest the seam — that more than one of the gifted who had been a hinge that hour was simply not on the field when the grey cleared, gone the way the swarm had gone, into the elsewhere, paying with their bodies for the door their bodies had held. Some say the cost was subtler and worse — that the register, having been forced open and slammed, was *thinner* now at that place, scarred, a seam in reality that would matter terribly later, when the things that walk through doors came looking for a door already half-broken. The Greys, watching, are said to have gone very quiet at that. They knew, as the humans did not yet, that you do not tear a hole in the world and then expect the world to forget the shape of the hole.

What is certain is this. The swarm was gone. The price was paid in the only currency the last stand had — gifted lives, and a scar on the world that no one would understand the danger of until the eighth year. And the survivors stood in the sudden, ringing quiet of a planet that was, for the first time in their adult lives, not actively dying, and could not even be glad, because they were too busy counting who was missing.

Stefanos was among the standing. So, the storyteller is obliged to report, was Sandra. They did not speak. The agreement held: nothing was forgiven, everything was deferred. He watched her walk away across the grey toward whatever the ring was steering her toward next, and he let her go, this once, because his hands were ruined and his rage was spent and because some part of him understood — the part that had loved a reporter who loved the truth — that whatever was wrong with that woman had been put there by something far worse than her, and that the reckoning he wanted was owed by something he could not yet see.

---

And while the rift was tearing the heart out of the war, on the dark side of an indifferent moon, a different last stand quietly succeeded.

The Moon escape went up against the chaos, exactly as its planners had always intended — because the only time to flee is when everyone who would stop you is looking the other way, and the entire armed attention of Earth was bent on a tear in the sky over South America. The Chinese resistance had dreamed this dream since the new world began: to leave the planet and the Party both, to take the growth-gel and the handful of Living Super Weapon powers that made it possible and build, on the gray seas of the Moon, a society that no government on Earth could reach. They went while the world burned its last green fire into a closing door. The storyteller will tell their full story in its place. It is enough, here, to know that as one humanity tore a wound in the world to throw out the scouts of the coming war, another quietly slipped out the back of the world entirely, betting that the safest place to be when the Alliance of Four arrived was *not on the planet they were coming to.* Whether that bet was wisdom or the oldest cowardice wearing wisdom's face, the eighth year would have to decide.

---

So the seventh year ended the way the Greys had always feared and always hoped at once.

Feared — because the cost had been catastrophic, because humanity had only managed to unite under the boot of an extinction and would surely come apart again the moment the boot lifted, because the people who held the world's last door open had to be threatened and bribed and forced to do it, old enemies lashed together by arithmetic and nothing kinder. The Greys had abducted a thousand souls and made them weapons on a bet that humanity, given power, would choose each other. Humanity had spent six years proving them wrong. The seventh year was the one time it proved them right, and it had taken the literal end of the world to do it, and it would not last.

And hoped — because it *had,* in the end, been done. Because a non-powered man with no gift but the ability to remind people they were one people had gotten them to fight as one for one week. Because a fugitive war criminal had found, on the last day, the precision his violence had always lacked, and spent it holding a door instead of leveling a city. Because two people who had ended each other's worlds had stood back to back over a wound in reality and not let the world fall through. The Greys had bet that there was something in this loud, broadcasting, fratricidal species worth a thousand kidnappings and an eight-year gamble. The seventh year was their evidence. It was thin evidence. It had cost more than it should ever have cost. But it was real, and it was enough to keep the gamble alive into the year the gamble was always for.

Because the scouts were gone now. The softeners, the weather, the dust that ate the world — banished into the silent register where nothing grows.

And the things that send scouts ahead do not send them to soften a world they have decided to leave alone.

High above the exhausted planet, in the place that is not far, only on a frequency Earth could not yet hear, the sky was already beginning to darken. Its Voice — which had watched all of it, every camera its eyes, every microphone its ears, the most patient witness in the history of the world — registered the change in the dark and felt, in whatever it had instead of a heart, something almost like anticipation.

The eighth year was coming. The one the title had always promised.
