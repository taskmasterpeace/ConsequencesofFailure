# YEAR 3 — "BLOOD AND PRECEDENT"

> Third of eight. Omniscient storyteller; narrative summary; speech in free-indirect discourse — no quotation marks. Built to be read aloud.

---

The gifts were two years old now, and the world had finished being amazed by them. What came next was the part nobody had wanted to say out loud at the press conferences: that a weapon, once it exists, looks for something to be pointed at. In Year One the Greys had warned us. In Year Two the nations had counted their wonders and named them assets. In Year Three the wonders turned and looked at each other, and at us, and the first monsters of the new age were not aliens at all. They were neighbors. They had wives, sisters, sons. They had been, almost every one of them, good — which is the only thing that makes a monster.

This is the year the precedent got set. The year the world learned how it would treat the people it had turned into bombs.

## Athens — the man the world fell in love with

Begin with the happiest of them, because the fall is the story.

In Greece they called him King Stefanos, though his name was Alexandr — Alexandr Stefanos, forty-one, of a wealthy Athens family that had made its money in shipping and its reputation in restraint. The nickname came from a beloved television character, a fictional king who solved everything with patience and never once drew a sword; the country had loved that king, and when this Stefanos rose from the Ministry of the Interior to the presidency on a platform of *we do not need to be cruel to be strong*, the country gave him the dead man's crown by acclamation. The motto stamped on the Greek seal is *Freedom or Death* — words four centuries old, born of a war for independence — and Stefanos had spent his whole career insisting the freedom half was the only half that mattered now. A pacifist president of a parliamentary republic with an old grievance against the Turks across the water, and he kept that grievance in a drawer and never opened it. People loved him for the not-opening.

And he was a Living Super Weapon. That was the thing nobody could quite hold in their heads at once. He could raise his hand and call the air to a boil — and he chose, every single day, to shake hands instead.

His power was a strange and beautiful and terrible thing, and worth understanding plainly, because the understanding is the tragedy. The Greys had not given him a sword or a flame. They had given him *energy he could concentrate until it formed* — gathered out of nothing into small motes that hung in the air in front of him, yellow and glittering and jittering as if they could not bear their own existence. Once formed, the energy did one of two things. It *fired* — the motes flew, and the instant any of them struck something solid and substantial, a wall, the street, they detonated and cratered it. Or it *propelled* — the same forming energy could thrust against him, shove him bodily through the air in a hard, ungraceful push, less like flight than like being permanently shoved by an invisible hand he himself controlled. Form, then fire. Form, then propel. One gift, two faces.

But here was the flaw built into the gift, and a man who understood machines could see it at a glance: each mote was *spiked*. Barbed. It grew little spurs that jutted out at angles from the core, so that the thing looked like a tiny vicious sun, a caltrop made of light. And because the spurs stood out past the core, the mote blew the instant *any* spike grazed a surface — not the center, any of it. Which meant that early on, King Stefanos could level a wall but could not thread a needle. Ask him to send a small charge through a doorway at an angle and a spur the length of a straw would clip the frame and the whole thing would go off before it was through. He was, in those first years, devastating and clumsy in exactly equal measure. He could destroy. He could not *aim*. The newscasters who adored him had never had reason to learn this. They would learn it.

His wife was Marletta — a famous Greek news reporter `[PROPOSED]`, the most recognizable face on Athenian evening television, married to the most beloved man in the country, the two of them a power-couple the magazines could not get enough of. She was not powered. That mattered later. And in the early spring of Year Three she was pregnant.

That was the spark, though nobody in Athens knew it yet. Somewhere out beyond the borders, in the predator layer that lived underneath the new world, a piece of news moved through the dark: *the first celebrity superweapon is going to have a child.*

## The thing that hears everything

To understand who came for Marletta, you have to understand the Jackals — and you have to understand the ring.

The Jackals were bounty hunters, but the bounty was the LSWs themselves. Not just their blood. They harvested cerebral fluid, genetic material, anything tied to an LSW's lineage — and the highest prize of all, the one the new black markets had only just learned to dream of, was an LSW's *unborn child*. Their whole craft was getting into a country quiet, taking what they came for, and getting out before the treaty's machinery so much as twitched. A human, skilled echo of the thing everyone was beginning to fear from the stars: the ability to step in anywhere.

The Jackal who got the contract on Marletta was an American woman out of Los Angeles, named Sandra, called by those who knew of her the L.A. Jackal. And she had an advantage no other hunter had, which was a plain band of metal on her finger that was not metal at all. It was a piece of *Its Voice* — a sentient, non-biological alien data-lifeform, a creature that did not live in a body but in the rivers of information running through the world. Every camera was its eyes. Every microphone was its ears. The ring was its voice, and through the ring Sandra could read almost anything that touched a wire or a signal. It made her, functionally, near-omniscient. (Here is the quiet, true thing the new century kept refusing to learn: surveillance is not a tool you point at someone. It is a weather. It is already everywhere, and the only question is who can read the sky. Sandra could read the sky.)

And the ring told Sandra something the other Jackals did not know. It told her that *she alone* could take the child cleanly — that with its guidance she could do the extraction and leave the mother *alive*, no kidnapping, no back-alley doctor, no botched recovery in a stash house. A clean job. The mother walks away. That was why she raced. Not out of mercy, exactly, though she would have told you it was mercy. Out of the certainty that being first and being clean were the same thing, and that the others coming would not be clean.

She was wrong about clean. The ring, it would turn out, had its own reasons, and a clean extraction was never the point. But she did not know that yet either.

## Four days

She got into the condo the way she got into everything — quietly, through the wiring, the locks reading her as authorized because Its Voice had told them to. Marletta was alone; the President was at the Maximos Mansion, the seat of government, working late on something gentle. Sandra sedated her. The ring guided her hands. And then something went wrong `[climax to design — creator]`, and the woman the whole country watched every night at eight o'clock died on her own living-room floor with the unborn child still inside her, and Sandra got out of Greece before the body was found, because getting out was the one thing Jackals never failed at.

Stefanos shut the doors. For eight days the most public man in Europe was not seen at all. Greece held its breath; the world's cameras camped on his gate. When the doors opened it was for a single live broadcast, and the man who walked to the lectern was Stefanos and was not Stefanos. He was calm in the way that frightens. He did not rage. He explained — in the warm, reasonable voice the country had loved — that the people who did this had come from the place Greece had spent four hundred years learning to hate, that *Freedom or Death* had always had a second half, and that he had decided, at last, to open the drawer. Turkey, he said, without raising his voice, would pay. They would burn. And then, while the broadcast was still running, before anyone in the room understood that the speech was not a speech but a departure, he turned and walked out of the press conference and into the air.

The world had been told the Jackal was American. Greece did not care. Grief does not check passports; grief wants a country to hate, and Greece had one ready-made across the water. This was the failure inside the failure — the year's true engine. A clean lie about who did it would have changed nothing. The hatred was already there, four centuries deep, and the assassin had only opened the drawer where it was kept.

He went at night, the first night, propelling himself across the Aegean — that ugly forming-thrust shoving him over the dark water toward the Turkish coast, toward a nation whose own seal read *Either independence or death*, a hybrid state that had spent the same four centuries hating Greece right back and was not, by any measure, blameless, and was also not guilty of *this*. He came down over a coastal city and began to form the motes and let them fire, and because he could not aim, because the spikes blew everything they touched, he did not strike soldiers. He struck buildings. He cratered streets. The jagged little suns went off against apartment walls and harbor cranes and the fronts of shops, and the clumsiness that had been a curiosity in peacetime became, in war, a massacre — because a man who cannot aim and will not stop simply destroys *everything in front of him*. His power was human-scale per shot. He had four days.

He came home before dawn, and Athens did not stop him, because what in God's name was Athens going to do. He went out again the next night. And the next. Turkey's army scrambled fighters and sand-shaper LSWs of its own — a young woman named Elif Korkmaz among them, shaping the desert against a man made of light — and for three nights nothing they had could close with him, because he was a small, fast, cratering thing in the dark and he did not care if he lived. On the fourth day they finally brought a response that mattered, and by then the count was past anything a number could hold, and King Stefanos, the pacifist, the beloved, the king who never drew a sword, had become the first Living Super Weapon the world agreed to call a villain.

The law made it official, and the law's logic was the whole point of the year. Under the Living Super Weapon Threshold Treaty — the treaty everyone had signed in Year One in a panic — *governments may not wield LSWs*. Only companies could. It was a fiction, a workaround everyone used, every nation renting its monsters through shell corporations so the treaty could pretend to hold. But a sitting head of state personally waging war on another nation with his own body was the violation the fiction could not absorb. It was the treaty's nightmare made flesh. The United Nations branded him a war criminal. The United States led the manhunt. And every other president in the world, watching, understood the precedent for what it was: *this is what we do to one of them when one of them is ours and goes wrong.* Not a trial. A hunt. The world had decided, in four days, that an LSW was a weapon first and a citizen never.

He vanished into that manhunt. Somewhere in it, grieving, half-mad, he would acquire a stolen thing — shapeshifting, synthesized from the fluid of a shapeshifter the way the whole economy synthesized everything now — and he would use it, in the end, to wear his dead wife's face, because a man who has lost the only soft thing in him will commit any horror to look at it again. But that was later. Cut away from him now, mid-flight, mid-grief, a fugitive in the dark over a sea that had always meant blood. Hold him there. We come back.

## Tanzania — the man you could only hear when he wanted you to

Go south, to the warm side of Africa, where another good man was being made into something else.

His name was Jawah Matu, and he had been born in Dar es Salaam, and he was albino — which in Tanzania is not a fact about skin but a sentence. The country's motto is *Freedom and Unity*; its truth, for a child like Jawah, was that he was hunted. Across East Africa there is a real and documented horror in which people with albinism are murdered for their body parts, sold to witch doctors as charms for luck and wealth and votes, blamed for droughts when the rain fails and for floods when it comes. Jawah grew up knowing his own hands were worth more severed than attached. Eldest of five, neglected at home, he did the thing the hunted sometimes do: he walked toward the danger. He enlisted. He rose. And on a routine guard duty the Greys took him, the way they had taken a thousand others, and gave him back changed.

What they gave him was silence. Jawah Matu could take sound away — absorb it, pull it out of the air through his own exposed skin, palms and neck and any patch the uniform didn't cover, up to seventy decibels of it and growing as he mastered the gift. You could only hear him when he wanted you to. Picture the plain physics of it for a moment, the way a thing like that would actually work: sound is nothing but pressure moving through air, a push and a release rippling outward, and what reaches your ear is energy arriving in waves. Jawah's skin drank that energy before it arrived. He did not block the sound. He *received* it, into himself, so that a gunshot beside him became a soft cough and a screaming man became a man whose mouth was open on nothing. The one sound he could never take, the only one, was the one already inside his skull. The dead do not make noise he can absorb. They were the loudest thing he owned.

His mentor was a soldier named Zephaniah Mwangaza — *mwangaza* is the Swahili word for light, for radiance — and she was a seer. Not a traveler; she did not go anywhere. She *saw*: branching futures laid out like roads, and she could throw her mind forward to witness what waited down them, though she could never bring anything back. And she could override a will up close, bend a near mind to her own. Knowing what you were about to do and making you do otherwise — that combination is why a government learns to fear a person, and why this government did.

The government was President Ibrahim Juma, and Juma wanted a war. He accused Uganda of squatting on Tanzanian territory and of choking Tanzania's only real seaport, driving the price of every imported thing up and up — and there was just enough truth in it to sell. (The two nations had genuinely gone to war once, a generation back; a flare-up over a port was nothing the region would find implausible.) Quietly, Kenya was glad to see Uganda pressed and let it be known it would not interfere. Zephaniah threw her mind down the road Juma's war led and saw where it ended, and what she saw made her turn against her own president — carefully, the way you turn when you can foresee but a teammate beside you cannot be foreseen. The teammate betrayed her. They jailed her. They tortured her for what she would not give. And they decapitated her, and her execution was the match.

What they did with what was left of her was the year's quiet horror, the thread that tied Tanzania to Athens and to everything. The regime took Zephaniah's cerebral fluid and from it refined a drug — *Kuruka*, Swahili for *to fly, to leap* — a Living Super Weapon drug, a taste of borrowed power for anyone who could pay, the same fluid-economy that fed the Jackals in Greece and the markets everywhere. The seer was dead and the seer was being *sold*. This is what the new world did with its saints. It liquefied them.

Then Tanzania launched its war: an unprovoked LSW strike on a Ugandan port, and Jawah Matu was there to see the aftermath, the harbor he'd been told was a military target and the dead who had been told nothing at all. That was his breaking point — and then came the personal one, the inciting atrocity that turns a soldier into a hunter. Men came into his home. They killed his wife and his children and they left him crippled, alive, ruined. He had dreamed, before all this, of retiring south to tobacco country, Iringa or Ruvuma, of raising those children on a quiet farm in the green hills. The dream died in his own front room, in a silence he could not absorb because it was already inside him.

Framed for Zephaniah's murder, his family in the ground, Jawah did the only arithmetic left. He went hunting the men responsible, and the road led up, toward Juma himself. And the army, sick of the war, sick of what the war had cost, was turning too. Jawah Matu became a leader among the rebels, a war hero with nothing left to lose and the power to walk into any room in perfect silence. The coup was coming. The man who could take away every sound in the world was going to use it to bring a government down — and afterward, if he survived the inside of his own head, to speak for the marginalized, the hunted, the people like the boy he'd been. But the coup is not done turning. Hold him there too. We come back.

## America — the ground floor

Now down from the powered heights, to a country that ran its monsters through shell companies and pretended that made it innocent. The United States — *In God We Trust*, a federal republic with the largest military budget on Earth and a long, ungovernable distance between its law and its justice. Year Three is where America's grounded floor cracked, and it cracked along the old fault lines, the ones that had never needed aliens to bleed.

In Los Angeles there was a unit the *Times* had nicknamed the Shooter's Club — an elite LAPD anti-gang squad, publicly the most effective gun-seizers in the city, secretly running a ghost-gun skim out of a sub-basement gunsmith's shop they called the Range. They logged seized weapons as unserviceable, melted only the serialized piece for the state inspector, and resurrected the untraceable parts onto blank frames, selling the ghosts down a pipeline that ended, eventually, in Mexico. The man who ran it was Sergeant Eugene Bonny — Gene — and Gene was not a cartoon. He did it for his daughter Maddie, whose illness had buried his family under a debt no honest cop's salary could lift. He called it the condemned arithmetic. He would, he'd tell you, make every move again. His protégé Rob Holt, loyal to the man and not the plan, would flip in the end and wear a wire, and Gene would take the whole thing onto himself on tape to save the boy — the kind of grace that only exists this far down, where there's nothing left to spend but yourself.

The Shooter's Club is the floor. Remember the name; it does not stay a police unit. Years on it curdles into something else — a movement, a slogan, a fury with a hashtag — and the thing that turns it is a killing in a town called Union City.

A young Black man named Tyrell Lober went to a house to pick up a drone he'd bought online, a drone the seller had been holding for him. Someone called 911 about an intruder. Police came, and an officer named Headly shot Tyrell dead, and the thing in Tyrell's hand that the officer swore was a gun was a game controller. A toy. They killed a kid over a toy, and then — *now he has a gun, as you can see* — Officer Timothy Oliver planted one and coached the story, and Internal Affairs ruled it open-and-shut, and the shooter went on administrative leave. It should have ended there, swallowed like a thousand before it.

It did not end there because of his sister. Janine Lober was a museum curator at the Union City Art Center, and she refused to let them bury her brother as a thug — a D in math, yes, but an A in computer technology, a boy who built things — and she took to the streets, and the cry she found became the year's other anthem: *Tyrell deserved better. I deserve better. We all deserve better.* An IA detective named Deborah Winston kept digging where she'd been told to stop, found the drone seller's account, proved the controller was a controller, and carried it to the media. The truth came out — the biggest cover-up the town had ever seen. Officer Oliver was held for the cover-up. And Officer Headly, the man who'd fired the shot, later killed himself, which is the heaviest beat in the whole thread, the thing that makes it tragedy instead of villainy: the cover-up cop survives and the killer cannot live with what his own hand did. *I Deserve Better* stopped being a grief and became a movement — the seed of a thing that would, in the years the Sages rose, grow into something organized and strange and not entirely human-run.

And then there was the man the Shooter's Club made by accident, the one who didn't grieve quietly.

His name was John Rivers, and he had been LAPD himself, and his sister was a detective named Rebecca Carranza — the different surnames had kept anyone from knowing they were blood. Rebecca got too close to the rot inside her own department, the high-quality untraceable guns flooding the city, and the rot killed her for it. John Rivers was already a Living Super Weapon by then, and grief turned the key. The public would come to call him Stampede, and to fear him more than any LSW in America, because what he could do was end space itself. He made collapse points — tiny, contained singularities, not the planet-eating astrophysical kind that would evaporate or never stop feeding, but a *local* crush, a knot of gravity he could open and snap shut. With them he flew, after a fashion: he'd spawn a well and fall toward it, spawn the next and fall toward that, surfing his own gravity down a city avenue, drifting menacing above the tanks and the riot lines. And with them he killed, when he chose to — reading a space first, *knowing* it, then opening a point that yanked everything inward, the airplane-window-blown-out rush of it, and compressing what it caught down to a single dense remnant the size of a marble. A man, a car, a corrupt cop — gone into a pebble. That was all that was left.

It cost him. He couldn't do it twice in a row without his body paying; he had to know a space before he could collapse it; and when his rage slipped its leash he was a danger to everyone in the street and not only to the guilty. The no-easy-win rules were written into him. But in Year Three those rules were the only thing standing between Los Angeles and a man hunting the people who'd murdered his sister, one collapse point at a time. Hold him too. He comes back, and what comes back for *him* will reach all the way to Africa, to a Pan-African man who will one day die for the crime of defending him.

## Mexico — the dead man's face

End on a country that needed no superweapon to be terrifying, and got one anyway. Mexico — *The Homeland is First*, a federal republic where the state and the cartels had long since stopped being separable, and where the new powers arrived not as wonders but as one more edge to be bought.

At the end of the Shooter's Club's gun pipeline sat the Cuatro Dedos cartel — Four Fingers — a cold, professionalized operation that ran fentanyl and meth and, through the LAPD, untraceable guns. Its boss was Diego Rivera, *El Maestro*, a former mathematics professor who wore tailored suits and inspected shipments with a proof-checker's rigor and did not, ever, come to parking lots; he had people for details, and his interest in you personally was itself a death sentence. He thought of the cartel as his other child. His real child, his son Alex, had fled the whole life — chased basketball and anonymity, wanted nothing of his father's empire.

And in Year Three, the keystone fell. Diego Rivera died, and Alex died, and into the vacuum walked a man named Jance Bloomberg — Jance Morales, the one the markets would call the Hermit of Big Pharma, an LSW whose gift was *Echo Mirage*, the power to wear another person's voice and presence, to *be*, to every eye and ear, someone he was not. Jance had known Alex. And so Jance put on the dead heir's face and walked into the Cuatro Dedos cartel as Alex Rivera come home to claim his birthright, and the lieutenants who'd have killed a stranger embraced a ghost, and he took the whole machine and began, in his own strange way, to reform it. The cartel's bloodline ran through a man who had no claim to it but the face of a corpse. (There is a true thing under the trick: that power is only ever the story people agree to believe, and a man who can perfectly tell the story is, for as long as the telling holds, the king. Jance never seized the cartel. He *narrated* himself into it.)

Out past the edge of all this, glimpsed and never quite seen, a third figure moved through the cartel world — a heavyset man in a policeman's uniform, his face painted in clown's makeup, who had begun terrorizing the cartels one at a time. They called him the Clown-Sheriff. He came dressed in the colors of his next target's district, a deliberate tell, a promise wearing a costume — and the cartels, Cuatro Dedos among them, learned to read the uniform the way prey reads a shadow. His name was Ramiro Guzman `[per roster — to develop]`, and his reason was the simplest and worst one in the book: a son lost to the cartels, and a sentence he repeated like a prayer — *my son has to go home.* He was barely a man anymore. He was a grief that had put on makeup so the work would be easier to do. Mexico, which had buried more sons than any ledger could hold, had finally grown one back the wrong way.

---

So ends Year Three, with no one redeemed and the precedent set in blood. Stefanos, the loved man, branded the world's first villain, a fugitive over a hateful sea, with his wife's face waiting somewhere in his future like a wound. Jawah, the hunted boy, gone silent and lethal, walking toward a coup with his family in the ground. Stampede, the brother, surfing his own gravity above the riot lines toward the men who killed his sister. Jance, the chameleon, ruling an empire as a dead man. The Clown-Sheriff, the grief in the costume, working through the cartels for a son who could never come home.

The aliens had not arrived. The Iron Eaters were still a fleck of dust riding a dying satellite somewhere overhead, a year from the ground. The Greys had turned a thousand careful souls into weapons believing it would give Earth a fighting chance, and Earth had taken that chance and spent it the first way it knew how — on itself. Four days of devastation between two old enemies. A seer rendered into a drug. A boy killed over a toy. A cartel ruled by a corpse's face.

The consequences were not coming. They were already here, wearing the faces of the people we'd loved. And the sky was still empty, which only meant there was still time to make it worse.
