# YEAR 4 — "THE IRON EATERS"

*Consequences of Failure — the novel, first draft. Part Four of the eight-year countdown.*
*Style: third-person omniscient; narrative summary; speech in indirect / free-indirect discourse — no quotation marks. Built to be read aloud.*

---

It had been falling for eleven years before anyone on Earth knew enough to fear it, and even then they feared the wrong thing.

The satellite had a name once. It was a communications relay the size of a delivery van, built in a clean room in a country that no longer maintained it, lofted into a medium orbit to bounce television and telephone across an ocean, and then forgotten when the company that owned it folded and its successors decided the cost of de-orbiting a dead machine was greater than the cost of pretending it was not their problem. So it stayed up there, tumbling end over end in the long cold, a coffin of aluminum and gold foil and dead solar wings, one of some thirty thousand pieces of human junk that crowd the sky and that the people below almost never think about. Space, the listener should understand, is not empty. The orbits nearest Earth are a slow-motion junkyard, a shell of abandoned hardware turning at seventeen thousand miles an hour, and the only reason it does not constantly collide with itself is that the volume up there is enormous and the things in it are, by cosmic standards, very small.

The dust rode on the underside of one of the dead solar wings. It was not human dust.

How a fleck of anything survives the fall to Earth is worth a moment, because everything that follows turns on it. When a satellite's orbit decays — and every orbit decays; even at that height the thin breath of the upper atmosphere drags on a thing for years, stealing a little speed each pass until the floor comes up to meet it — the machine does not glide down. It hits the air at orbital speed, and air at that speed is not air. It is a wall. The leading face compresses the gas in front of it so violently that the gas glows, three, four, five thousand degrees, hot enough to boil iron, and the satellite comes apart in a streak of fire that anyone beneath the track would have mistaken for a generous meteor. Almost all of it burned. The gold foil flashed to vapor. The aluminum ran like candle wax and shed itself in burning droplets across forty miles of upper sky.

But heat is a thing that takes time to travel into a body, and the fall is fast — ninety seconds, no more, from the top of the fire to the ground. A small enough mass, riding in the wake of the larger one, in the shadow of the bow shock where the air spills around rather than slams into, can come through the gauntlet scorched but whole. Spacecraft engineers have a word for it: they call the surviving pieces the *debris footprint*, and they will tell you that the densest, most stubborn fragments — a fuel tank, a reaction wheel, a flange of solid steel — routinely reach the surface intact. The dust was smaller than any of those, and tougher than all of them, because it had not been built to fly. It had been built to *land.*

It came down at dusk, on the fourteenth of a wet month, over the green immensity of Brazil.

---

The state of Pará is a place the size of three Frances laid edge to edge, most of it forest, the Amazon running through it like a brown sea with a current. The fragment fell west of Marabá, into the broken country where the great forest has been gnawed back into pasture and cattle and the rusting bones of failed gold mines — *garimpos*, the wildcat diggings that have poisoned a thousand creeks with mercury and left the land pocked and scarred. A rancher named Dário Sousa saw the light. He was on the porch of a house his grandfather had cut out of the mata with a chainsaw and a grudge, and he watched a green-white line draw itself down the eastern sky and break apart into a scatter of smaller embers, and he thought, the way half of humanity has always thought at such a sight, of a wish. He did not make one. He had stopped wishing some years back, around the time the price of beef and the price of his debts crossed each other going opposite directions.

What he found the next morning, when he rode out to where the cattle would not graze, was nothing he could have wished for. A patch of his pasture the size of a tennis court had gone the color of ash and rust, the grass standing dead and gray, and in the middle of it the barbed-wire fence that had marked his western line for forty years was simply *not there.* Not cut. Not stolen. The wooden posts stood as they always had, weathered and leaning, but the wire that had run between them — strand after strand of galvanized steel, three hundred meters of it — had been reduced to a fine reddish powder that lay along the ground in a perfect line, as though someone had drawn the memory of a fence in rust and walked away.

Dário did the sensible thing, which was to assume someone had cheated him, and the wrong thing, which was to crouch down and run the strange powder through his fingers. *(This is the part the storyteller asks you to hold onto: he touched it. We will come back to him.)*

The thing in the powder did not eat flesh. It had no use for him. It was looking for metal.

---

A self-replicating machine is the oldest nightmare in the engineer's toolbox, and the most elegant, and the Iron Eaters were the purest expression of it that the world had ever seen. To understand them you must first set aside the picture the word *nanite* paints — the tidy silver mote, the obedient little robot. These were not robots in any sense a human factory would recognize. They were closer to a mineral that had learned to be hungry: crystalline things smaller than a bacterium, each one a scrap of structure and a scrap of instruction and almost nothing else, and what the instruction said was very short. *Find metal. Take it apart. Use what you take to make more of yourself. Repeat.*

Why metal, and why first? Because metal is where the energy is. The listener handles steel and aluminum as if they were free, but they are not free; they are expensive. To pull iron out of its ore takes a furnace and a fortune in heat, and all of that invested energy stays locked inside the finished metal as the orderly grip of its atoms upon one another. To a thing that can pick those bonds apart one at a time, a steel girder is not a building material. It is a battery, fully charged, left lying in the street. The Iron Eaters cracked the bonds, drank the energy, and spent it building copies of themselves out of the atoms left over — and then those copies fanned out and did the same. The mathematics of it is the simplest and most terrible kind. One becomes two, two become four, four become eight, and the line between *a curiosity in a Brazilian pasture* and *a continent's worth of plague* is only a question of how many times the doubling has happened. It is the reason a single contaminated thing can sicken a world: nothing in nature multiplies as fast as a number multiplying against itself.

And it is the reason the infrastructure went first. People imagined, later, when they imagined it at all, that an alien plague would come for *them* — for the body, the blood, the brain. The Iron Eaters had no interest in any of that. They went, with the blind certainty of water running downhill, toward the richest seams of refined metal humanity had ever piled up: the power grid, the railways, the pipelines, the machines. The towers that carry the high lines. The reinforcing bars threaded through every bridge and every tall building like a hidden skeleton. The world had spent two centuries smelting itself a nervous system of steel and copper and aluminum, and it had built that nervous system everywhere, and it had never once imagined that the metal itself could be *eaten.* The Iron Eaters were the answer to a question no one had thought to ask. They were the advance scouts of an army that had not yet come — the softeners, the wave any patient invader sends ahead to take the legs out from under a world before the world even knows there is a war. But the people of Pará did not know any of that. They only knew that the lights had begun to flicker, and that the flickering was spreading.

---

It spread the way a stain spreads on a tablecloth, slow at the edges and then all at once. Within a week the transmission line that carried hydroelectric power north out of the Tucuruí dam — one of the largest dams on Earth, a wall of concrete holding back a lake you could see from orbit — developed a fault no engineer could locate, then a second, then a cascade, and the lights went out across half a state. The crews who climbed the towers to find the break found instead that the towers were *crumbling*, the lattice steel gone soft and red and powdery to the touch, sloughing rust in the wind, and one of them, a lineman from Belém named Caio who had climbed steel for nineteen years, put his weight on a crossmember that should have held a truck and felt it give like wet bread, and fell, and lived, and would tell the story for the rest of his life with the particular flatness of a man who has touched something that should not exist.

The Brazilians, to their lasting credit, were among the first to take it seriously, and the storyteller will not let the listener pass over *why*, because it is the kind of truth the country wears on its own flag. *Ordem e Progresso* — order and progress — runs in green letters across the blue globe at the center of the Brazilian banner, a phrase borrowed from a French philosopher by men who believed, in the optimistic morning of the republic, that a nation could be engineered like a bridge. It is a beautiful, brittle motto for a country that has never quite had either thing reliably, and there is a melancholy in it that every Brazilian carries somewhere — the gap between the order on the flag and the disorder of the day, between the progress promised and the potholes delivered. So when the metal of the nation began to die, it struck at something deeper than infrastructure. It struck at the story the country told itself.

President Lucas Mendes — a careful man, a federal republic's careful product, who had reached the Planalto by offending the fewest people rather than inspiring the most — went on the national broadcast and asked his people to remain calm, and the broadcast itself stuttered and dropped because the transmitter's antenna array was, even as he spoke into it, quietly turning to dust. It would have been comic if it had not been the beginning of the end of so many things. He convened the army. He convened the Ministry of Strategic LSW Development, and its quiet, formidable chief, Dr. Isabela Varga, who had spent four years building Brazil's careful, banned-on-paper-and-everywhere-in-practice apparatus for the powered, and who now found herself with a crisis no power on her roster had been designed to fight — because how does a man who can crush stone, or a woman who can drown a city, fight a thing too small to see, that turns his own rifle to rust in his hands?

It was Varga who first said the word *quarantine* about a place rather than a person, and Varga who first understood — staring at the satellite imagery of the dead zone spreading out of Pará in a ragged ring — that they were not dealing with a chemical or a fungus or a weapon any nation had built. The dead zone grew at a rate that no spill could explain. It grew the way a *thing alive* grows. And things that are alive, she knew, can sometimes be made to serve.

---

Half a world away, in a glass tower in New York that bore no name on the door because the man who owned it preferred it that way, Charles Sapphire was given the same satellite images at almost the same hour, and looked at the spreading rust, and felt something move in him that he would have been ashamed to name.

Charles Sapphire was not, by any ordinary measure, a happy man. The listener who has followed this story from its beginning knows the shape of him: a tech billionaire of mixed white and Black blood, raised wealthy in New York, blessed and cursed with a mind that learned faster than minds were meant to — he had built a speed-reading method as a boy that he later called BrainTrain, and he had carried that prodigious appetite into MIT and a friendship that had curdled into the central wound of his adult life. He had loved a woman, Emily Rhaige, an aspiring actress with a philosophy she wore like a charm — *life is like a deck of cards* — and he had kept that love a secret, and then the terror group Akrahuhum had killed her in an attack he could not stop, and the grief had not made him gentle. It had made him an army. Deck 52 was that army: a private military company structured like a deck of cards, with its four suits and its dealer at the top, born of vengeance and a religious man's terrible certainty that the world's evil could be answered with enough force, applied precisely enough.

And Charles had a weakness, the kind that wears the mask of a strength. He could not look at a problem without taking it apart to see what made it work, and he could not see a thing that worked without wondering what it would do if he aimed it. His company's great invention, RoboForge, was the proof of it — a marriage of artificial intelligence and nanotechnology that could take raw material and shape it, molecule by molecule, into a working machine on command. It had made him richer than Emily's killers had ever dreamed of being. But it was crippled, and Charles knew exactly how. The nanites he had built could *shape* metal beautifully, the AI drawing the blueprint and the little machines laying down the matter, but they could not *feed* themselves; they ran down like a watch, slow and short-lived and forever tethered to a power source, brilliant in a factory and useless on a battlefield. RoboForge could build the body of a thing. It could never give that body a fire in its belly.

He looked at the images out of Pará — at a machine that ate metal and turned that metal into limitless power and limitless copies of itself — and he understood, with the clean awful clarity that was the worst thing about him, that the world's new plague was the exact missing half of his life's work. The Iron Eaters were the furnace he had never been able to build. They were the hunger his beautiful, bloodless tools had always lacked.

His head of the Spades suit, the technology division, was a woman named Dr. Lena Cho, and she had a hunger of her own — a transhumanist's faith that the line between the human and the made was a wall to be torn down rather than a fence to be respected. When Charles told her what he wanted, she did not flinch. She told him it could kill them all. She told him that fusing a self-replicating metal-eater into RoboForge was like teaching a fire to cook for you, and that fire does not stay taught. And then she told him she would need a sample, and a deniable team, and the part of the dead zone the Brazilians had not yet sealed.

Charles authorized it before she finished the sentence. He told himself it was defense — that someone would weaponize this thing, and better the man who would aim it carefully than the men who would not. It was the same thing he had told himself about Deck 52. He had a gift, his enemies would have said, for being the hero of his own grief.

---

Deck 52's Aces went into Pará in the dark, in unmarked aircraft, against the standing order of a sovereign nation, and the storyteller will not pretend the operation was clean, because it was not. The infiltrators wore no metal — that was Cho's first hard rule, learned from the lineman and the fence: leave the steel at home. They went in with carbon-fiber blades and ceramic and weapons of polymer and rope, into a stretch of dead forest west of the Itacaiúnas river where the dead zone had swallowed an abandoned *garimpo* whole, the mining machinery standing in the clearing like the rusted skeletons of dinosaurs, sagging into the powder of themselves. They took their sample there, from the still-active edge of the rust, in a containment vessel made of diamond-coated glass because there was nothing else the Eaters could not chew. They lost a man on the way out — not to the Brazilians, who never knew they were there, but to a single mote that had gotten into the seal of one operative's vessel and into the buckle of another's harness, and turned a climbing rig to powder a hundred feet up. He fell the way the lineman Caio had fallen, but he did not live.

And so the sample reached the Spades labs, and Dr. Lena Cho began the work that would make Deck 52 the most feared private power on Earth and would brand it, in the eyes of millions, as something worse than feared. Because here is the irony the listener should carry forward like a coal in a closed hand: Charles Sapphire was weaponizing the very plague that was eating the world. The thing crawling through Brazil's power grid, the thing that would soon be crawling through every grid on Earth, was about to become the engine of his private army. RoboForge version two was the marriage Cho had warned against and built anyway — RoboForge the architect, drawing the blueprint; the Iron-Eater nanites the furnace and the feedstock, devouring metal for power without limit and laying it down again in any shape the AI desired. Married, the thing that had been useless in war became unstoppable in it: fast, self-feeding, lethal, a machine that fed on the battlefield it fought across and grew stronger the more steel its enemy brought. Beyond human, the new motto would say. Beyond boundaries.

The world would not thank him. The world, when it learned — and it would learn — would call it collaboration. It would say the Dealer had cut a deal with the invaders, had taken the scouts' own weapon into his hands and turned it on his fellow men, and the accusation would not be entirely wrong, and that was the part Charles could not bear and could not stop. There is a kind of man who can only love the world by mastering it, and who discovers, too late and over and over, that mastery and love are not the same thing.

---

But the Iron Eaters, for all the terror of them, were a knowable terror. They ate metal; you could starve them, seal them, study them, and — Charles had proven it — even harness them. They had a logic. The thing that came out of a sealed laboratory that same year had no logic at all, and it is the second story this Part must tell, because the two of them together — the plague of the machines and the plague of the flesh — are why the listener will remember Year Four as the year the ground itself began to give way.

It was not aliens that made the second plague. That is the bitter heart of it. It was *us.*

There were, by the fourth year of the eight, a great many people in the world trying to understand the Living Super Weapons — the roughly thousand human beings the Greys had taken and remade and returned, each carrying a power the world had not asked for. And there were a great many people trying to *make more of them*, because every nation and every company that held one wanted ten, and the Greys had stopped giving. The Sages — those uncanny few who carried the complete knowledge of a single field of study, planted whole in their minds — included among them a woman named Cissy Oliva, a Ugandan who had become the wealthiest person in Africa on the strength of carrying the entire science of genetic engineering behind her eyes, and the work she could do, others were trying, clumsily, to copy. The dream was simple and old as Frankenstein: if a power lived in the body, then the body could be read, and what could be read could be written, and what could be written could be *given.* Take two LSWs whose gifts ran deep in the blood. Find what in their cells made them what they were. Splice it, culture it, deliver it. Manufacture a hero.

The experiment that broke the world was run not by Cissy, who knew better, but by people who had only watched her from a distance — a program inside one of the powers, the kind of program that lives in a building with no name and answers to no committee, that pulled two superpowered subjects into a sealed facility and went looking in their genomes for the thread of the miracle. The storyteller will spare the listener the particular faces in the room, because they did not survive to matter, but will not spare the principle, because the principle is the warning. They took genetic material from two LSWs whose powers should never have met, and they built a vector to carry the spliced result into a living body — and a vector, the listener should know, is most often a *virus*, a tamed and hollowed-out virus, because a virus is the one machine in all of biology whose entire purpose is to get inside a cell and rewrite it. That is what a virus *is*: a set of instructions in a protein shell, knocking on the door of your cells, and your cells, helplessly hospitable, letting it in and doing what it says.

What the cells were told to do, this time, no one had intended.

The spliced power did not knit cleanly into a human frame the way it had knit, by the Greys' impossible craft, into the two it was stolen from. It tore. It expressed wrong — the body trying to be two incompatible kinds of weapon at once, the gifts at war inside a single skin, and the result was not a hero and was not a corpse. It was something between, something that should not be able to live and lived anyway, hungry and wrong and no longer the person it had been. And the tamed virus, the vector, the thing whose only purpose was to get inside cells and rewrite them — that part worked perfectly. It was, by ghastly accident, contagious. It got out of the body it had been put into and it went looking for more, and what it found first, by the same dark logic that drew the Iron Eaters to refined metal, was the richest possible material for it to work on.

It found the other Living Super Weapons.

For now — only for now — it could not touch ordinary people. Their cells did not have the strange architecture the spliced instructions needed to grip, the way a key cut for one lock will not turn another, and the world would draw a desperate, doomed comfort from that fact in the months to come. The first to turn were the powered. A man who could crush stone became a thing of crushing hunger. A woman who could drown a city became something that walked out of the sea changed. The very people every nation had been hoarding as its shield — its thousand precious weapons — began, here and there, in ones and twos and then in clusters, to sicken and twist and stop being people. And no one, at first, connected the monster in the sealed facility in one country to the dead grid spreading out of a pasture in Brazil, because there was no connection — except the one that mattered most and was hardest to see. Both were the same lesson, written twice. The world had been handed powers it did not understand, by hands that meant well and hands that did not, and the world had reached for them all the same — to eat the plague, to manufacture the miracle — because that is what the world does. It reaches. It does not wait to understand.

---

The Part closes where it must, on the small human hinge that the great machinery so rarely shows.

Dário Sousa, the rancher who had run the strange rust through his fingers on the morning after the falling star, did not turn into a monster, because he was no LSW; he was only a tired man in debt. But the dead zone reached his house in the end, as it reached everything made of metal, and on the night he abandoned the land his grandfather had cut from the forest, he stood in the doorway with a kerosene lamp because the wires in the walls had gone to powder, and he looked back at the rooms going dark, and he thought — in the way a man thinks the truest things only when no one can hear him — that he had touched the thing, that first morning, before anyone knew to be afraid, and that nothing had happened to him at all. He had been spared. He did not yet understand that being spared and being saved are not the same, and that the powder he had let run through his fingers was already, that night, dusting the high steel of every city in the hemisphere.

Far to the north, in a laboratory that smelled of ozone and money, Dr. Lena Cho watched a fragment of stolen alien hunger crawl across a sheet of titanium and devour it and bloom, obedient now, into the first perfect RoboForge soldier of the second generation — and she felt the awe that is the most dangerous feeling a clever person can have, the awe that forgets to be afraid. And in another building with no name, in another country, a sealed door held back a thing that had been a person, and the seals were good, and the seals would hold for a while, and a while is all the world ever gets.

The metal was sickening. The flesh was sickening. The scouts had landed, and done exactly what scouts are sent to do, and the army they answered to was still four years from the sky.

And the Greys, who had turned a thousand human beings into weapons in the hope that those weapons would make the world strong enough to stand — the Greys watched the world turn those very weapons into the source of a new horror, watched it eat itself with the gifts it had been given, and could only wonder, in whatever passes for sorrow in a thing that travels between dimensions, whether they had armed a child or handed a child a knife.

The doubling had begun. In both plagues, the doubling had begun.

And the listener, who knows what a number does when it multiplies against itself, knows already what the fifth year is named.
