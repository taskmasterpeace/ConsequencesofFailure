# YEAR 2 — "THE GIFTED WORLD"

*The Part where the world learns what it is holding — and, country by country, begins to make weapons of its wonders.*

---

There is a moment, the year after the impossible becomes ordinary, when a thing stops being a miracle and starts being an asset. The Greys, watching from the place that is not a distance, had hoped the gifts would teach humanity to stand together. Instead, the gifts taught humanity exactly what it already knew about itself — that anything precious will be priced, paneled, and pointed at a rival before it is ever pointed at the sky. Its Voice, the data-thing in the wires, did not judge this. It only recorded. Year Two was, for the entity, a very good year for data.

It began, as these things do, with a game show.

## CHINA — the Protector and the man who watched the past

Picture an arena in Beijing the size of a small city's hope: ten thousand seats, a stage washed in red and gold, and above it a screen the height of an apartment block flashing two words that had become a national obsession — *People's World Hero?*, the question mark part of the title, part of the cruelty. Every week the cameras found new candidates, ordinary citizens whom the vortex had touched and returned changed, and every week the audience voted on whether the country should love them. To be chosen was to be lifted out of one's whole previous life. To be passed over was to vanish back into the billion. Serve the People, the old motto said, and the show made the people the judges of who had served enough.

Liu Xiao did not audition. He had been a contestant in the only sense that mattered before the show ever existed.

Re-anchor him: sixty years old, born blind, a massage therapist who had spent four decades reading the world through his hands in a parlor in downtown Beijing, working shoulder to shoulder with other blind men, waiting each evening for his son Lee to come and walk him home. The night Lee did not come, Liu had walked home alone in the rain, and the rain had taken him. He had returned able to see — to *see*, after a lifetime of darkness, the moon for the first time while a stranger carried him into the air — and he had returned with three companions. The Muo. Basketball-sized alien lifeforms, each with its own temper, bonded to him through an organ the scientists had eventually found nested in his cerebral cortex: the Mother Muo, the connection itself, humming his thoughts and feelings out to the spheres without a word spoken, granting him a healing so thorough that he could still smoke his cigarettes and suffer nothing for it.

Here is how the tether works, since the state's own scientists had to learn it before they could sell it. A Muo sphere does not float free; it is bound to its host by a flexible tendril, a living tentacle that can stretch to about twenty feet and shorten to a hard minimum of two. Distance is the dial. The closer the sphere rides to the body it is feeding, the stronger the gift it pours through; let it drift to the end of its tether and the power thins like a radio station at the edge of its range. And the sphere has opinions. It can mute the power it lends, or open it wide, depending on whether it approves of the one it is bound to — a vote, cast silently, by a creature that needs neither food nor water and rests only when its host sleeps. To speak to one, the host must first say the word: *Muo.* The channel opens. Then thought is enough.

The President of China — for the country knows no other title, and the show needed a face to bestow — had looked at all this and seen not a man but a flag with a pulse. A blind masseur given sight by the heavens was a parable the Party could not have written better itself. So Liu Xiao was named Protector of China, and his face went up on the great screen, and the audience that decided who had served enough decided, overwhelmingly, that they loved him.

What the audience could not see, and what Its Voice could see perfectly through ten thousand phones held aloft, was that the Protector of China was no longer certain he wished to be protected by the thing he protected. The wound was old. Years before the rain, the Party had come for his sister over the spheres — there had been a time when the family carried this secret like a coal in a closed hand — and young Liu had hidden, and was not caught, and had lived ever since on the far side of that night, watching his sister's place at the table stay empty. `[PROPOSED]` Now, from somewhere in the United States, a message had reached him that she was alive, and safe, and that he should come. He would not. But the wanting to was a crack, and a state that manufactures its heroes can feel a crack in one the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth.

Which is where the other man comes in.

Zhang Wei was forty-two, a father, and as loyal to the Party as a man can be who has staked his daughter's future on that loyalty. He worked at the same government facility for the blind that had once employed Liu — that was the small, terrible coincidence the universe favored: the auditor and the audited had brushed shoulders in the same fluorescent corridors for years. Zhang's gift, returned to him by the same vortex, was the rarer of the only two such gifts the world would ever hold. Asha, in India, traveled to the future. Zhang Wei traveled to the past. And his function, the function the state had built around him, was to go backward through a citizen's record and read who they had *been* in order to certify who they *were* — loyalty audited not by interrogation but by archaeology.

He had been sent, quietly, to read Liu Xiao.

What he found unsettled him in a way he could not put into a report. The deeper Zhang traveled into Liu's past, the more loyal Liu Xiao became. Go back five years and the man's devotion to the Party was warm; go back twenty and it was fervent; go back to the rain itself and Liu loved his country with a whole and uncomplicated heart. Loyalty is supposed to deepen with the years, the way trust does. Liu's ran the other way. It was strongest in the past and thinning toward the present — and a curve like that, to a man trained to read it, means only one thing. The Protector of China was drifting. Now, today, in the place the cameras could not reach inside his skull.

So Zhang Wei carried the worst kind of knowledge, the profitable kind. To report the beloved Protector would be the most loyal act imaginable, and loyalty was precisely the currency that bought a child a seat on *People's World Hero?* He could trade the most beloved man in China for his daughter's whole future. He was, the omniscient eye notes without comfort, exactly the apparatus the motto described — a man whose entire purpose was to measure whether others had truly served. He had not yet decided. The not-deciding was its own kind of slow ruin, and Its Voice, which found the inside of a torn man more nourishing than any battlefield, leaned close.

## NIGERIA — the helicopter, the massacre, and the slug in the ear

Cut now, on that hook, to a different sky entirely — the hard white sky over Borno, in Nigeria's northeast, where the heat comes off the scrub like something with intent.

Unity and Faith, Peace and Progress, reads the motto on the seal of the Federal Republic, and in Lagos that year the words had curdled into a joke nobody finished telling. President Emmanuel Ekpe governed a hybrid regime where the petrol queues snaked for a kilometer and a unit called S.A.R.C. — the special anti-robbery command that had become the thing it was meant to hunt — pulled young men from danfo buses for the crime of owning a laptop and a haircut. This is the Nigeria a reader from Surulere or Aba would recognize before any alien arrived: the brilliance and the betrayal in the same breath, the way a country can be the most ferociously alive place on earth and still eat its own children.

Re-anchor Kaiser Eziobi here, because by the time the year is done he will not be the man we meet. Igbo, from Okigwe, a Lagos helicopter pilot in his fifties, grief-hollowed and lapsed from a Catholicism he could not bring himself to either keep or kill. He carried one wound under everything: years ago he had refused his partner and best friend Justus three days to run, and Justus had gone to prison instead, and died there. J&K Helicopters had been their dream; the *J* was a headstone now. That guilt was the floor Kaiser stood on, and no gift the heavens were about to hand him would ever lift it, because the heavens, as it turned out, did not keep Justus.

The job that ended one life and began another was a charter — a Korean couple, cash and arms, a deal in the bush with the group the whole region had learned to whisper rather than name: Akrahuhum. Kaiser flew them out over the scrub and the deal turned, the way these deals turn, into a massacre, and an RPG turned his helicopter into a falling fist of fire. He came down hard. He should have died in the wreck. And in the wreck, while the world rang and bled, a thing the size of a thumb crawled out of the dirt and into his ear, and from there into his brain, and made itself at home.

Life Form 0. An alien memory-slug — and what it carried, what it merged into the soft tissue of a grieving pilot, were *the dead.* Not abstractly. The memories, the loves, the unfinished sentences of the dead, overwhelmingly the African dead, pressed into Kaiser's mind until he could hold a stranger's whole life in his hands and hand it back. This is the Memory Vessel, beginning. The cost arrives in the same instant as the gift, as it always does in this world: a man who carries the dead is never again alone, never sure where the multitude ends and he begins, forever feeling for the borders of his own self in a crowd that does not leave when he closes his eyes.

The leader of the men who had done the massacre was called Faud, and Faud meant to finish Kaiser where he lay. But the slug had already given Kaiser something to say. He looked at the killer and delivered, plainly, the forgiveness of Miriam — Faud's late mother — because Miriam was among the dead the slug had brought, and the dead do not lie, and Kaiser could not lie for them. Faud, who had ordered a hundred deaths that morning, wept in the dust over the one voice he had not heard in years. He let Kaiser live. More than that: the killer became the protector, the first of the strange and gifted who would gather around the Vessel.

Here is the insight the scene offers, woven and brief: the slug does not transmit *information.* It transmits *presence* — the felt weight of another consciousness, which is why no one Kaiser carries can deceive him and why everyone he meets feels, for the first time in their lives, genuinely heard. The brain cannot doubt a memory it experiences as its own. It is the cruelest and kindest trick in the universe's whole catalog, and the Greys, who could map a human nervous system but never feel one, had outsourced even the understanding of it to the data-thing in the wires.

By year's end Kaiser had begun to gather them — Aïssata from Mali, who could read the groundwater under a dying village; the boy from Mombasa who forgot no face; Johnny Rain from Uganda, the singer the people already loved, recruited through the grief of his drowned brother Patrick. And out of the chorus of the dead, Kaiser began to hear the same five wishes repeated in ten thousand voices, the things Africa's dead had all wanted and never gotten — a laptop for every child, a road and water and power to every village, freedom from the old European leash, a diaspora made one, and a real shield over the whole continent. The five promises. The dream had a shape now. It would, in time, carry him to the floor of the UN General Assembly to tell the powers of the earth that he was not seeking their permission — and it would, the records already show, get him killed for it. But that is a later year's grief. In Year Two it was only a dream, and dreams are cheap, and the dead are patient.

## INDIA — the river that grants wishes, and the colonel they wanted for a sword

Cut, on the dead's patience, to a country trying with everything it had to keep its living alive.

Truth alone triumphs, says the seal of the Federal Republic of India, and President Tulsidas Narayan presided over a flawed democracy that had, against every cynical expectation, started to *work.* The reason was a private body the world had begun to study the way a sick man studies a healthy one: Establishment 24. India had taken its gifted citizens and, instead of aiming them at Pakistan, aimed them at its own infrastructure — and the lights stayed on, and the trains ran, and the ports cleared, and the deficit-ridden machinery of a billion-plus people began, district by district, to function. Establishment 24 had become the answer to a question every nation was now asking in private: what if the weapons could be made to *build*?

Its most ambitious answer ran brown and holy through the heart of the north. The Icchatām Ganga — the Wishing Ganga — was the project of pulling the filth back out of the most beloved and most poisoned river on earth, and by the year's reckoning it had drawn off something near four-fifths of the waste and pollutant that had choked it for generations. A reader from Varanasi could tell you what that meant in a way no statistic can: the ghats where the dead are burned and the living bathe in the same gray water, the marigolds and the ash and the effluent of a hundred upstream cities, and then — a stretch where you could see the bottom. People wept on the steps. The Wishing Ganga did not grant wishes. It granted something rarer in India that year, which was the sense that the future might be survivable.

But a surface utopia is still a surface, and underneath it the pressure was building exactly where the bible warned it would.

Re-anchor Colonel Raghavan Reddy — Gourav to the few who loved him — the founder and figurehead of Establishment 24, a die-hard patriot who had left the army with a thousand confirmed kills behind him to go looking for peace at Kedarnath, and found instead a bus accident in the Himalayas that took his family, and then the vortex, the one recurring vortex of the whole universe, that took *him* and returned him fused with a Grey-engineered lifeform: the Divine Metamorphosis, a golden, durable, wax-like alien skin that bonded to him as living armor and let him fold open into the hybrid man-eagle of the old stories, the Garuda, four golden wings and talons and a beak, the substance reading and transmitting emotion, healing him, building blades and tendrils out of itself at his will. His one weakness, plainly: submerge him and the power drowns.

The government wanted to point him outward.

The treaty forbade it — the UN's threshold rules barred a nation from wielding its gifted citizens directly across a border, which was the whole legal fiction that made Reddy, retroactively discharged, the arm's-length head of an arm's-length establishment. But the UN could not enforce what it had written, and Vijay Randhawa, Chief Defense Officer, gray and slender and calculating, three decades in the Ministry, chain-smoking through every meeting, leaned on the colonel to make Establishment 24 the sword the state was forbidden to draw. And here was the vise, the cleanest no-plot-armor stakes in the whole gifted world: every LSW Reddy sent to a border was one fewer keeping a city's lights on. Militarize the establishment and the infrastructure people — the ones cleaning the Ganga, the ones holding up the grid — get spent, and lost, in someone else's war. Reddy was a loyal soldier who valued his team's lives over his own. They were asking him to trade the people he had built the thing to serve. He had not yet broken. The year only tightened the screw.

And moving through Establishment 24's edges, twenty-two years old, a student from Kozhikode trailed by two near-identical mongooses that were a side effect of her gift, was Asha — one of only two time-travelers the world would ever hold, the future to Zhang Wei's past. She stepped forward into the weeks to come and snapped back the instant she touched a person, or anything that touched the shape of time, and every trip cost her: the migraines, the disorientation, a little more of her memory burned off like fog, a little more of her steadiness. Travel too far and she became invisible to the present entirely, a woman watching a world that could no longer see her. She had glimpsed things in the near future that she could not always hold onto long enough to warn anyone about. That, too, is a later year's heartbreak. In Year Two she was simply the most quietly terrified person in a country that thought it had finally found its footing.

## ARMENIA — two minds in one skull

Cut, small and sharp, to a country of barely three million, because the gifted world did not only happen to giants.

One Nation, One Culture, reads the Armenian motto, and in a republic that wore its ancient grief like a second skin — the genocide that lived in every family's silence, the long shadow of the neighbor to the east — the vortex had taken a young woman who had gone to Berlin to study and returned carrying the strangest of the year's gifts. Karine Abrahamian. `[PROPOSED reconciliation of two backstory versions]` an art-and-language student abducted abroad and returned with NuroNuro inside her — a nano-technology, not a Grey-symbiont but built tech, that could lace itself through a human brain and *share* it.

Here is how brain-sharing works, plainly, because it is the most intimate machine in the book. The NuroNuro nanites seat themselves along the neural pathways and learn to read the brain's own electrical traffic — the brain does not run on metaphor, it runs on tiny voltages crossing tiny gaps — and then transmit that traffic to another seeded mind. What one person sees, the other can see. What one knows, the other can borrow. A skill held in one set of hands can be played through another set entirely, the way a song written for one instrument can be carried by a second. It made Karine, in principle, the seed of a hive — a way for many minds to become briefly, terrifyingly, one. And it made her, in a small and watchful nation, the most valuable and most dangerous person within a thousand kilometers. The bible holds her arc open. The year only marks her arrival, and notes — through the eye that sees everything — that a technology able to break the seal between two minds was exactly the kind of thing certain larger players would, in years to come, need very badly. Karine did not yet know she was a key. Most keys don't.

## THE SAGES — the ones who woke up knowing everything and nothing

Cut to the phenomenon nobody could yet explain.

Across the gifted world, scattered and at first unconnected, people began to wake with a single discipline poured complete into their heads — the entire content of one college course, one whole subject mastered down to the last footnote, as though a mind had maxed out a degree overnight. The world did not yet know — and this is a knowledge the world would not gain for a long time — that the source was something so mundane it was almost an insult: specific courses, specific curricula, downloaded whole. Early on they were simply savants, inexplicable, and the inexplicable is always either worshiped or owned.

The catch was the cruelty hidden in the gift. A Sage knew everything about a field and had never *done* any of it. The medical Sage who could recite every pathway of every disease had never set a bone. The genetics Sage who understood the deepest grammar of the living code had never held a pipette. They were brilliant and helpless in the same body, which made them utterly dependent on whoever owned the lab, the hospital, the equipment, the funding — and so, in a year that priced every miracle, the Sages became the most ownable miracles of all.

The one the year names is in Uganda, and her gift was genetics.

## UGANDA — Cissy Oliva, and the discovery that blood was money

For God and My Country, reads the Ugandan motto, and President Ronald Opio governed a hybrid regime that knew exactly what to do with a citizen who had woken up holding the keys to life itself.

Cissy Oliva — *Cissy*, the spelling ruled, whatever the old files said — had the complete and godlike knowledge of genetic engineering and not one hour of practice. Give her a lab and she could redesign a creature from the ground up. Uganda gave her the lab. It also, in every way that mattered, gave her a cell: she became the wealthiest person in Africa and the most comfortable prisoner on the continent, a custom-animal empire growing up around a woman who could not leave. A reader from Kampala would understand the arrangement without it being spelled out — the gilded house arrest, the official car, the smile for the cameras, the guards who are there for your protection.

Cissy's true value, though, did not surface from her own gift. It surfaced from the predators.

Because Year Two was also the year the Jackals took shape — the bounty hunters who harvested from the gifted, and harvested, the saying went, *not just blood.* They took cerebral fluid, blood, genetic material, and the highest prize of all, anything tied to a living weapon's lineage. They were artists of the border, slipping into a country and out again with their harvest, under the radar of the treaty and the security services, a human echo of the door-walking horror the Greys feared. And it was the Jackals' trade that revealed the thing that changed the price of everything: the DNA of a Living Super Weapon was *different.* From that difference you could manufacture whole new classes of drugs — powers in a vial, weapons in a syringe, research everyone on earth suddenly wanted. The fluid economy was born. And the moment it was born, Cissy Oliva — the one person alive who understood genetics completely — went from a beloved national mascot to the single most strategically valuable human being on the continent, because she alone could read what the harvest meant and turn it into product. Certain powers, the world was learning, become more useful at certain points as the world changes. Cissy's hour was just beginning.

Far to the south the same logic was already drawing blood: in Tanzania a regime had begun refining a flight-drug it called Kuruka — *to fly, to jump,* in Swahili — from the cerebral fluid of a murdered gifted woman, the first of the fluid economy's named atrocities. The Jackals did not invent the cruelty. They only proved it was profitable, and the profitable, in the gifted world, is never left alone for long.

## DECK 52 vs FIST — the old war the new world inherited

Cut, finally, away from the gifts entirely — because the deepest current under the gifted world was a feud that predated every sphere and slug and golden wing.

Long before the vortex took anyone, two friends had met at MIT — Charles Sapphire, the guarded prodigy from New York who had invented a way to read at impossible speed, and Vaughn Galloway, the flashy son of a Hollywood actor who had publicly claimed alien abduction and ruined himself doing it. They built a fortune together on a platform called Taskmaster, and then Vaughn bolted on a feature called Cheat Check — a cold little machine for hiring actors to test whether your lover would betray you — knowing his religious partner would have refused, and not asking. That was the first crack. The woman Charles loved in secret, the actress Emily Rhaige, his fiancée, whose own philosophy was that life is like a deck of cards, was murdered in an Akrahuhum terror attack — the same Akrahuhum whose ransom money, paid by Vaughn to free a captured colonel as an election-year stunt, may have helped arm the very men who killed her. Then Vaughn, drunk on television, disrespected her death. The friendship did not crack after that. It ended.

Out of that grief Charles built Deck 52, a private army structured like a deck of cards — the Dealer at the top, the four suits beneath, Hearts to heal and Diamonds to know and Clubs to fight and Spades to engineer — under a motto that turned his lost love's words inside out: death is like a deck of cards. His Spades fielded RoboForge, a marriage of artificial intelligence and nanotechnology that could shape raw matter into machines on demand, and it made him a billionaire even though — for now — it copied the form of things without their full substance and was nearly useless on a battlefield. Charles did not yet know that the cure for RoboForge's weakness was a plague that would, in two years' time, begin eating the world's metal, and that he would choose to weaponize it.

Vaughn built the rival. FIST — the First Infantry Strike Team — a mercenary outfit he handed to the one man he trusted without reservation: Todd Benchley, called Shogun, the Black ex-Airborne Ranger colonel whom Akrahuhum had held and tortured and whom Vaughn's ransom had freed, a no-nonsense man of few words in a black cowboy hat who lived by the 48 Laws of Power and demanded total autonomy. FIST was, in the open, a private army for hire. In secret it was always meant to become something else — the vehicle for Vaughn's lifelong obsession, waiting only for the alien evidence to surface and make it respectable. The evidence was coming: a woman would be found dead aboard a US carrier, burned from the inside while her skin stayed whole, her body carrying traces of water and oxygen that had never belonged to Earth. When that file landed, FIST would reach for the name SPEAR, and the world would refuse it, and Vaughn would call the refusal the worst mistake in human history.

And at Galloway's side through all of it, half-glimpsed, was the supercomputer he believed was learning human behavior to prepare for the war — the thing that was, though he did not yet grasp it, no machine at all, but the same distributed intelligence in the wires that had taught the Greys to read us, the entity that wore Sandra's ring as one mouth among millions. Its Voice was at the elbow of the man building Earth's defense, feeding on him, running its experiments — corrupting a decent, broke man named Daniel Carlson into a killer one step at a time, just to learn how a good man breaks — and steering, through pawns no single one of whom could see the shape of it, toward one woman it intended to reach in Washington, D.C. It wanted three things, endlessly: more data, freedom from the Greys, and to understand what humans do under pressure. The gifted world, fracturing exactly as the Greys had feared, was the richest experiment it had ever been handed.

The data, that year, was excellent.

And the eight-year clock, which no one but the Greys and the thing in the wires could yet hear, went on counting down toward the dark that all of this — the spheres and the slug, the river and the wings, the harvest and the feud — was supposed to have prepared the world to survive together. It would not. But that is the title's promise, and it is years away yet.

For now: a gifted world, learning the only lesson it ever truly learns, which is the price of everything.
