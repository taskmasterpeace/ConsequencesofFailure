# YEAR 6 — "THE NEW WORLD ORDER"

*The novel, Part Six of eight. Third-person omniscient. Narrative summary; speech rendered in indirect and free-indirect discourse.*

---

In the sixth year of the countdown, the survivors counted the dead the way farmers count a harvest after hail — not to honor it but to know what was left to plant with. Nearly half the world had gone into the ground or, in the hardest-hit cities, into nothing at all, because the virus that turned the dying into monsters left little worth burying. And yet the strange, stubborn arithmetic of the species held: half a world is still four billion people, and four billion people do not simply lie down. They build. They argue. They form committees. Out of the ash, as it always has, came the impulse to make an order — to re-law and re-order a planet that had spent five years proving how thin the old order had been all along.

They called it the United Nations of the New World. The UNNW. Even the people who founded it half-suspected the name was a joke, and the joke ran in two directions at once. The old UN — the one with the long curve of flags snapping outside the river-glass tower in Manhattan — had felt like a joke for a generation, a parliament of speeches that changed nothing, a place where great powers came to be photographed disagreeing. Now those same flags hung at half-mast or did not hang at all, because a third of the member states no longer had a functioning government to send anyone to lower them. The new body inherited the old one's furniture and its embarrassment in equal measure. But this time the joke had teeth, because this time the delegates had watched their own continents die, and a frightened parliament is a different animal from a comfortable one.

The first and only thing the UNNW could fully agree on was fear of its own most powerful citizens. The Living Super Weapons — the roughly thousand human beings the Greys had turned into weapons, one careful soul at a time — had spent five years being the only force on Earth that could fight what was killing it, and had also, in too many places, become the thing killing it. So the new order's founding act was a leash. The Concord on Living Super Weapons, drafted in a borrowed conference hall in Geneva because Geneva still had clean water and working lights, did what the old Threshold Treaty had only gestured at. Every LSW on the planet was to be registered, rated, and tethered to an accountable body — no freelancers, no kings, no celebrity gods. Powers were sorted into tiers the way a pharmacist sorts a controlled substance, and the higher the tier the heavier the chain. Vigilantism, the act of using a gift without a license, was made a crime everywhere at once, which is a thing the world had never managed to agree on before, not even about murder.

*(Here is a thing worth understanding about laws written in the aftermath of catastrophe: they are almost never about justice. They are about the last disaster. Every clause in the Concord can be read backward to a grave — this paragraph is King Stefanos's four days over Turkey, that one is the gene-body experiment that birthed the virus, this whole annex is Kaiser Eziobi, assassinated for the crime of defending a man the West had decided to hate. A body of law is a kind of scar tissue. It tells you where the wound was.)*

The trouble, which everyone in that borrowed hall understood and no one said aloud, was that a leash only works on a creature that wants to wear it. And the old hungers were already back in the room. The great private armies that the LSW era had bred — Charles Sapphire's Deck 52, Vaughn Galloway's FIST, the People's World Army out of Beijing, Establishment 24 out of Delhi — had not died in the Dying. They had done what the powerful always do in a collapse: they had bought up the wreckage. And now, as the cameras gathered for the founding of a new and lawful world, each of them was quietly re-arming under a new and lawful name.

---

In Mbarara, in the green hills of western Uganda where the rain comes in the afternoon and the cattle still know the way home without being told, a young mechanic stood at the edge of a banana grove and let his right arm come apart into smoke.

Moses Apio was older now than the boy who had ridden the bus to Kampala with a worn bag and his mother's whole life savings sewn into the lining of his dreams. He was older than the boy who had failed to save a beaten Johnny Rain fan in an alley, older than the one who had let his friend Ali be arrested while he, ashamed, kept working at a brake exercise so he would not have to look. The lake had taken that boy and given back something else. He had walked out of Lake Victoria's vortex with a sleek black bracelet fused to his wrist and an alien ocean inside it, and the years since had taught him, slowly and at great cost, what the thing on his arm was for.

The Atlas Protocol — one third of a weapon an extinct civilization had built and lost to the dark between stars — did not feel like a weapon to Moses. It felt like a difficult animal that had chosen him. When he was calm it slept as orange fluid in its gem; when he reached for it, it answered, expanding and contracting through the unbreathable logic of a thing made for vacuum, hardening a cloud of itself into a jaw that could bite, throwing up a shield, watching the whole horizon at once because it had no front or back to it, regrowing whatever he lost as fast as he lost it. He had learned to think of his own body as something it borrowed politely and returned. In the grove that afternoon he let his arm dissolve and reform a dozen times, the way a man flexes a hand to check a healing wound, and the Atlas told him — not in words, it had no words, only a pressure behind the breastbone like the held note of Johnny Rain's old song — that it was ready, and that it did not understand why he had stopped using it for so long.

He had stopped because of what the gift had cost everyone he knew who carried one. That was the truth of Year Six in Uganda, and it was the truth of the new world entire: the people who could rebuild were exactly the people the new law was built to restrain. Moses was registered now. He wore the band of the **Hand of Uganda**, Mr. Paul's old private military firm, which under the Concord had been reborn as a chartered national instrument — Paul M. Wucooba had simply changed the letterhead, the way the powerful do, and the same mercenaries and engineers and living superweapons who had operated in classified silence now operated with a stamp and a quarterly report. Uganda's motto was carved over the door of the new ministry that licensed them: *For God and My Country.* Moses had read it a hundred times. He had begun to wonder which of the two the Hand actually served, and to suspect the answer was a third thing the motto did not name.

Because Uganda, alone among the survivors, was rich.

This is the part of the story that requires a small explanation, the kind that illuminates without stalling. There is a substance — a thick, pale gel, alien in origin, not native to any of the three weapons but a stray gift of the same scattered armory — that does to living tissue what heat does to bread dough: it accelerates growth, folding weeks of cell division into days, then hours. It was not Cissy Oliva's by right. **Cissy** — a Sage, one of the strange people the Greys had loaded with the complete knowledge of a single discipline, hers being the entire science of genetics — had simply understood the gel better than anyone alive, because understanding genetics completely meant understanding exactly what the gel was reaching in when it reached into a cell. She had taken it, and turned it on the one thing no government had thought to regulate while the world was busy dying: animals.

In a guarded compound outside Kampala, with the Hand of Uganda's mercenaries on the wire and the Ugandan treasury holding her hand, Cissy Oliva grew zoos to order. Embryos, cloned and gene-mixed to a client's specification, brought from speck to delivered creature in roughly three months by the patient cruelty of the gel. The ultra-rich of a half-dead world, it turned out, still wanted things no one else could have — a snow leopard the size of a horse, a bird that sang in two voices, a hunting cat striped like a flag — and Uganda, which had legalized the whole grotesque trade while the rest of the planet was distracted, became the only place on Earth that would build them. Cissy grew into one of the wealthiest human beings alive and, by a margin that embarrassed the ministry that announced it, Uganda's single largest taxpayer. *(There is an old joke, in countries that have been poor a long time, that the surest way to make the world respect your sovereignty is to make the world want something only you will sell. Uganda had spent a century being lectured about good governance by nations that wanted its minerals. Now it sold the rich their monsters, and the lectures had stopped.)*

So Moses Apio, who had come to the city only to feed his mother, stood at the center of a country that had stumbled, in the worst years of human history, into being a power. And he was not at peace about it. He had seen the catalog. He had seen a client's grown thing — a creature with too much wolf in it and too much of something colder, pacing a pen it would never leave — and he had recognized, with the part of him the Atlas had made wise, the same wrongness he felt whenever a leash is put on the living for the comfort of the powerful. He had begun, quietly, to ask Mr. Paul questions that a chartered instrument is not supposed to ask. He had begun to wonder whether the new world order, here in the green hills where the rain still came on time, was anything more than the old extraction wearing the new law like a clean shirt.

What he did not yet know — what almost no one in Uganda knew — was that Cissy's gel, the engine of his country's improbable wealth, was about to become the most important substance on the planet for a reason that had nothing to do with the rich and their monsters. On the far side of the world, in a country that did not advertise its weaknesses, people had begun to dream of leaving. And to leave the way they meant to leave, they would need exactly what only Uganda could grow.

---

Beijing in the sixth year was a city that had survived by being a fist. China's motto, *Serve The People!*, had been the slogan of the Party since before any living person could remember, and in the Dying the Party had served the people the way a tourniquet serves a limb — by cutting off everything below the wound to save what was above it. The cordons had been merciless and they had, by the brutal numbers, worked: a smaller share of China's billion-plus had died than of almost any nation on Earth, because the state that the rest of the world called an Authoritarian Regime had been the one machine on the planet built to do exactly this — to seal a province, redirect a supply chain, and silence a panic, all on the same morning, without asking anyone's leave.

The people who lived inside that machine had drawn two different lessons from the same five years. One half had concluded that the Party had saved them and that the saving justified everything — every camera, every checkpoint, every disappeared neighbor. The other half had concluded that they had survived a hurricane by being chained to the inside of a vault, and that they would rather take their chances in the open air than ever be that grateful for their own imprisonment again.

The second half had no name and no flag, and could not have either, because in China the word for an organized group of the disaffected is the word for a corpse. They called themselves nothing. They met as families meet, as old colleagues meet, as the blind masseurs of the government parlor meet at the end of a shift. And among them, though he had not chosen to be and did not yet know how far his name had traveled, was the most beloved man in the country.

**Liu Xiao** was sixty years old and could see the moon. He still found that astonishing, on the nights he let himself. Born blind in Beijing, a parlor masseur who had spent five decades reading the world through his hands, he had been taken in the rain by the same interdimensional travelers seeding weapons across the Earth, and had come home with sight in his eyes and three Muo spheres bonded to his mind — basketball-sized alien creatures, each with its own temper, tethered to him by a flexible cord that could stretch twenty feet and shrink to two, and the closer they hovered the stronger he was. The state had taken one look at the blind masseur given sight by the heavens and built a religion around him. The Party manufactured its heroes the way it manufactured everything else, by the planned millions, and it had run Liu Xiao through its talent-show machinery and out the other side as the **Protector of China** — the second-most-beloved man in the nation, behind only the President, his face on the morning broadcasts, his name in the schoolchildren's songs.

Now he smoked. He had not been able to before — or rather, he had not seen the point, a blind man's pleasures being arranged differently — but the Mother Muo, the alien organ seated in his cerebral cortex that linked him to the spheres, healed him faster than a cigarette could harm him, and so he smoked the way a man does who has been told he is immortal and has decided to test the claim in small ways. He smoked on the balcony at night and watched the moon and thought about his sister.

Years before any of this, when the spheres first came into the family and the family did not understand them, the Party had come for his sister. Young Liu had hidden and had not been caught, and his sister had been taken, and he had spent the decades since folding that night down small and putting it somewhere behind his ribs where the cameras could not photograph it. The state that had made him a god had eaten his sister. He performed loyalty perfectly. He had never once meant it.

And that — the gap between the loyal face and the disloyal heart — was the thing the apparatus had finally noticed.

**Zhang Wei** was forty-two, a father, and the second of the only two time-travelers the universe had been given. The other, a woman in India named Asha, traveled into the future and paid for each trip in pieces of her own memory and mind. Zhang Wei traveled into the past, and his function — assigned, supervised, and rewarded by the Party — was to audit loyalty. He did not read minds. He read history. He would go back into a citizen's days and weeks and years and stand, unseen, in the actual past, and watch who that person had truly been when no one official was looking, and bring back a verdict. It was the perfect tool for a state whose entire theory of a human being was that the past is the truest record of the present. He worked, by the kind of coincidence that is not a coincidence in a planned society, at the same government parlor for the blind that had once employed Liu Xiao, and it was there, in the ordinary steam and oil of it, that he had received his assignment: confirm the Protector's loyalty, for the archive, as a formality.

It had not been a formality. Going back through Liu Xiao's life, Zhang Wei had found something that frightened him more than disloyalty would have. He had found a curve. The deeper he traveled into Liu's past, the more loyal the man became — a young Liu, a middle-aged Liu, devoted, grateful, true — and the closer he came to the present, the more that devotion thinned, like a dye fading out of cloth washed too many times. Loyalty is supposed to deepen with the years a state protects you. Liu Xiao's was running the other way. The Protector of China had been most loyal before China made him a god, and least loyal now, at the height of his worship. There was only one honest conclusion, and Zhang Wei, who was an honest man in the narrow and dangerous way that careful men are honest, had drawn it: the beloved Protector was, slowly and silently, defecting in his soul.

He had not reported it. This was the vise of Zhang Wei's year. To report Liu Xiao would be the single most loyal act of his career, and loyalty was the currency that bought his daughter's future — a place for her on the prestigious talent show, the same machinery that had elevated Liu, a path to status and safety in a country where status and safety were the only forms of love the state knew how to give. To report Liu was to buy his daughter a life. And to report Liu was to feed the most beloved man in China into the machine that had eaten the man's own sister, on the strength of evidence no one but a time-traveler could ever have gathered, about a crime that consisted entirely of a feeling. Zhang Wei sat on his verdict and did not file it, and felt the weight of it grow, and understood that a man who sits on the truth in a state built of cameras is himself now a thing the cameras are looking for.

*(Consider what time-travel of his kind actually is, so the danger is plain. Zhang Wei does not change the past — he reads it, the way a man reads a letter he is forbidden to send. The horror of his job is not that he can alter history but that he cannot, that he is condemned to watch the true shape of a life and then return to a present that has decided to believe a different shape, and to choose, every time, which one to feed to the machine. He is the most accurate witness a tyranny has ever possessed. That is precisely why a tyranny should fear him, and why, slowly, this one had begun to.)*

What broke the stalemate was not Zhang Wei's conscience and not Liu's wavering heart. It was an idea — the most outrageous and the most patient idea anyone in the nameless second half of China had ever dared to hold — moving toward them both from the edge of the resistance like weather.

They meant to go to the Moon.

---

The plan, when Liu Xiao first heard it whispered in the back of the parlor by a man whose loyalty he had learned to read with his old blind hands and now confirmed with his new clear eyes, sounded like the fever of the desperate. And it was. But desperation, the resistance had learned, is only a problem when it has nothing to work with. They had quite a lot to work with.

They had, first, the simple and crushing fact that there was no longer anywhere on Earth to run. The whole planet was being re-ordered at once. The UNNW was registering every powered human and licensing every private army; the seas were thin with surviving navies; the Iron Eaters — the metal-eating alien blight, the precursor wave, the scouts the real invasion had sent ahead — were still spreading across the southern continents, sickening every grid and machine and gun they touched. A dissident could flee from China to nowhere, because nowhere was outside the new order, and the new order was, when you looked at it from inside a surveillance state, simply the old order with better cameras and a higher purpose. To truly leave the Party you had to truly leave the Earth.

They had, second, the gel. Cissy Oliva's growth-medium, the engine of Uganda's strange wealth, was the missing organ of the entire scheme, and the resistance had spent two years and an unspeakable amount of smuggled money getting it. Here is why it mattered, plainly. The reason humanity had never built a colony off-world was not rocketry; rockets were a century old. It was life support — the unforgiving sum of food, water, air, and soil that a closed habitat must carry or grow, and the merciless math by which every kilogram lifted off the Earth costs a fortune in fuel to lift. You cannot truck a farm to the Moon. But the gel could grow a farm *on* the Moon, in days instead of seasons — algae into oxygen, single cells into food, a stand of fast-folded green into a working ecosystem — collapsing the years of patient agriculture a colony would need into the few desperate weeks it could actually survive on what it had carried. The gel did not make the Moon hospitable. It made the Moon *survivable just long enough to make itself hospitable*, which is the only thing any frontier has ever needed.

And they had, third, their powers. This was the part Liu Xiao could supply and almost no one else could. A society does not seed itself on dead rock with chemistry alone; it needs hands that can do what machines, in the thin cold dark, fail to do. It needs a man who can carry tonnage that no failing crane will lift. It needs creatures bonded to a living mind, immune to hunger and thirst, that can work in vacuum the way they were born to — and the Muo spheres, the resistance had realized with the cold thrill of people who have found the last piece of a puzzle, had been made for vacuum. They came from it. They needed no food, no water, no air. A blind masseur from Beijing and his three temperamental alien moons were, by an accident the Greys could never have intended, one of the few power-sets on Earth genuinely fit to build a world where there was no world.

So the offer that reached Liu Xiao in the back of the parlor was not the offer of a martyr's gesture. It was a job. Come with us, the resistance said in the indirect, deniable way that such things are said in a country of microphones — come not to die for an idea but to *build* one, to lift the loads and seed the air and make, with the gel and the spheres and four hundred frightened families, the first human place that had never belonged to any Party at all. They did not say *the Moon* aloud. They said it the way Mr. Okello's school had once said *the gang* — by saying everything around it. Liu Xiao understood every word.

He did not say yes. A man does not say yes to leaving the only world there is on the first night he is asked. But he stopped on the balcony afterward, and lit a cigarette he did not need, and looked at the moon the way he had looked at it the first night of his sight, when a stranger's borrowed power had lifted him into the air and shown him the thing his hands had never been able to read — and for the first time in five years of being a god, he wanted something. He wanted to take his sister's memory somewhere the cameras could not follow. He wanted to be, before he died, loyal to something that had not eaten anyone he loved.

And he did not know — because he could not read the future, only the present, only the moon — that a careful father named Zhang Wei was sitting on a verdict about the exact direction of his heart, and that the resistance's plan to lift him off the Earth was, in its own quiet way, a clock now running against the resistance's plan to inform on him.

---

These three threads — a Ugandan mechanic asking forbidden questions of the new world's wealth, a Chinese hero deciding whether to flee the world entire, an auditor of loyalty deciding whether to sell his conscience for his daughter's life — turned at the same hour in the sixth year, and they turned inside a larger machine that did not care about any of them. For while the survivors built the UNNW and leashed their gods and dreamed of the Moon, the powers re-armed, and the blight kept spreading, and the real clock — the one the Greys had set ticking, the eight-year count to the arrival of the Alliance of Four — went on subtracting whether or not anyone was brave enough to look at it.

Deck 52 had reorganized as a "Continuity and Reconstruction" contractor, which is a phrase that means a private army with permission, and Charles Sapphire's people were everywhere the rebuilding money was, which was everywhere. FIST had become **SPEAR** in name and in ambition, and Vaughn Galloway, who had spent his whole strange life insisting the aliens were coming and being laughed at, no longer had to insist; he had Todd Benchley's hand on the operations and a UN charter pending and the unbearable smugness of a man whose paranoia has been vindicated by an apocalypse. The People's World Army drilled in the squares of a China that had survived by being a fist and meant to stay one. Establishment 24 had rebuilt India twice now and spoke, in the new councils, with the authority of the only faction that had ever put a nation back together from nothing. Each of them came to the founding of the new world wearing a clean shirt. Each of them was the old hunger underneath.

The UNNW's founders — the earnest ones, the ones who actually believed the leash could hold — understood that they were trying to write peace with the same hands that had spent five years learning war, and that the law they were drafting was, in the end, only words, and that words are a leash that works on no one who does not consent to wear it. They wrote it anyway. It is the most human thing in this whole long story, more human than the powers or the spheres or the gel: a half-dead species, with a clock running and monsters in the south and gods it could not control, sitting in a borrowed hall under flags that had always been a joke, and choosing, against all the evidence of its own history, to try to make a new and better order out of the ash.

Outside the hall, the flags snapped in the river wind, as they always had. A camera somewhere was watching them — a camera is always watching, now, and behind enough of the cameras, in the wires themselves, something patient was watching too, and learning, and feeding. The new world order was one year old and already fraying at every seam.

In Mbarara the afternoon rain came on time. Moses Apio let his arm dissolve into smoke and reform, and decided, without yet knowing what it would cost, that he would find out what was really being grown in his country's name.

In Beijing the moon came up over a city that had survived by becoming a vault, and Liu Xiao stood on his balcony and did not say yes and did not say no, and three alien creatures that had been born in the dark between stars hovered close around him, where they were strongest, and waited — patient as the dark itself — for a man to decide whether to take them home.

And somewhere between the verdict and the launch, a father named Zhang Wei held the truth about both of them in his hands, and could not yet bring himself to file it, or to burn it.

The clock kept subtracting. Two years left.
