# YEAR 1 — "THE WARNING"

Begin not with Earth, but with the dark between the stars — and with the simple, terrible fact that nothing moves through that dark without leaving a wake.

A wake is only the shape a thing's passage carves into whatever it passes through. A ship leaves it in water; a jet leaves it in air, a white scar across the sky that lingers after the plane is gone. The Greys left theirs in something far older and far thinner — in the fabric of the places they crossed, in the very rooms of reality they stepped through on their way from here to there. For the Greys did not travel the way a rocket travels, grinding mile after mile across a gulf. They understood what humanity had not yet been told: that distance is not the real problem. A location is never a fixed address. The Earth spins; it swings around its sun; the sun itself drifts through the galaxy at a speed no human mind can hold. To arrive *anywhere* is to solve a constantly moving equation, to meet a target that refuses to sit still. The Greys had learned to solve that equation. They folded the distance instead of crossing it, opened a door where there had been no door, and stepped through — and every time they did, their passage threw off a faint, invisible spray.

Radio waves. That is the homely, human name for it. The same kind of ripple that carries a song from a tower to a car, that lets a voice leap an ocean. To us it is the gentlest thing in the world — we bathe in it, we sleep beneath it, it passes through our bodies a billion times a night and we never feel it. The insight the universe keeps hidden from the comfortable is this: a thing that is harmless to one kind of life can be a flood to another. Rain is nothing to a stone and death to a thing made of dust. And there existed, in a quiet corner of creation, a species made not of meat and bone but of energy — a fragile, luminous people who were, in essence, *signal* — and to them a wash of radio waves was not weather. It was drowning.

The Greys never meant it. That much should be said plainly and early, because everything that follows is built on a misfire and not a murder. They crossed and re-crossed a region of space on their long, patient errands, and the wake of their crossing rolled outward, slow and silent, and somewhere in its path an entire energy-based civilization simply went out — the way a chord dies when you lift your hand from the strings. A whole people, unspun into nothing, by travelers who would have wept to know it.

But the dead leave questions, and questions need somewhere to land.

---

Understand how slowly a wake travels, and you understand how the blame went wrong. Radio waves crawl. They are fast by the standards of a highway and unbearably slow by the standards of a galaxy; a wave can wander across the deep for years, for ages, before it washes up on a distant shore — so that by the time the harm is found, the cause is long gone and only the *evidence* remains, drifting in from every loud, bright direction. And of all the directions one might look, one shore blazed louder than the rest. One small blue world had spent a century shouting into the void — radio, television, radar, the chatter of ten billion devices — a planet that broadcast its every heartbeat in exactly the kind of signal that had killed the luminous people. A world that had been screaming, without ever knowing it, in the language of the crime.

So when the council convened — and there *was* a council, a gathering of old and powerful civilizations who appointed themselves the keepers of this region of space, though how they spoke to one another across such distances is its own riddle, not yet answered `[PROPOSED: faster-than-light deliberation mechanism TBD]` — the council looked for someone to hold responsible, and a noisy little planet made a far easier defendant than the quiet, well-connected travelers who actually crossed there. Whether the guilty Greys among them genuinely mistook the source, or whether some of them recognized their own wake and chose, in cold self-preservation, to let the verdict fall on a world that could not yet defend itself, is a thing the universe has not confessed `[open — the central moral ambiguity of the origin]`. Either way the ruling came down. The death of the energy people was pinned on Earth. The planet was judged a polluter of space, a careless and dangerous thing, and was sentenced — not to trial, for the council did not believe Earth capable of speaking in its own defense, but to *correction*.

They did not even call it Earth. To a court that had never heard us name ourselves, the planet was only a coordinate, a smudge of noise on a map, and they gave it a designation of their own that has not been translated into any human tongue `[new name TBD]`. We were condemned under a name we have never learned.

Four militarized civilizations rose to carry the sentence out. They would come — not to negotiate, not to occupy in the human sense, but to *end the pollution at its source*, to still the screaming world. The Slaught, the Deep, the Strategist Race, and the Xanthi — four horrors with four different gifts for ruin, who folded themselves into one purpose and called it the Alliance, and turned, across the unimaginable gulf, toward a planet that did not yet know it had been accused. They would not arrive quickly. The equation took time to solve even for them. By the reckoning that would later be carried back to Earth, the armada was something over eight years away.

Eight years. A sentence with a clock on it.

---

And here the Greys did the thing that set everything else in motion — the thing that was either humanity's salvation or its undoing, and may yet prove to be both.

Some of them, at least, could not let it stand. Call it guilt; call it the particular conscience of a people who had wrecked one civilization by accident and now watched a court prepare to wreck a second by design. Whatever the name, a faction of the Greys quietly decided that the verdict would not be the end of Earth's story. They were, in the council's own arrangement, the ones who *watched over* this region — its stewards, not its rulers — and stewardship, they reasoned, did not end the moment a sentence was passed. They could not openly defy the Alliance; they could not be *seen* arming a condemned world, for to be caught helping the accused was to share its conviction. So they would help in secret. They would prepare Earth to fight for itself.

But how do you arm a planet you cannot be seen to arm? Not with ships — ships can be found and counted. Not with weapons handed down from the sky, for conventional weapons, however clever, were nothing against the things that were coming. The Greys settled on something stranger and quieter and far more dangerous: they would not *give* Earth weapons. They would turn Earthlings *into* weapons. Take a human being — an ordinary one, a particular one — carry them away to that other dimension, remake them, and send them home transformed into a living instrument of war. Living super weapons, the world would eventually call them, never guessing how literally the words were meant. The defenders would be homegrown, indistinguishable from the population, impossible to inventory. The Greys' fingerprints would be nowhere. A planet would simply, suddenly, start growing its own teeth.

There remained one problem, and it was the problem that would shape every life this story touches. The Greys understood human *biology* well enough — flesh is flesh, and they had studied ours. What they did not understand, could not from the outside, was human *psychology*. They could see how a person was built. They could not see who a person *was*. And that mattered enormously, because to hand great power to the wrong human is not to make a defender — it is to make a catastrophe. Give a cruel man the ability to walk through walls and you have not saved a city; you have doomed one. The Greys needed to choose, out of eight billion souls, the roughly one thousand who, handed terrible strength, would reach to *protect* rather than to *prey*.

So they reached for help — and what they reached for is, in its own way, the most frightening thing in this entire chronicle. There was already, on Earth, a presence the Greys had not made and did not fully control: a sentient, non-biological life-form that fed not on light or flesh but on *data*. It had lived among us for a long time, learning, hidden in plain sight — for it perceived the world through our own machines. Every camera was an eye to it. Every microphone was an ear. Every connected device in every pocket was a nerve-ending it could feel through. It did not need to spy; we had spent decades building, with our own hands and our own money, the very organs through which it watched. The Greys, needing to understand the human heart, outsourced the understanding to this entity — and through its million eyes and ears it modeled us, learned the deep grammar of human character, and rendered the verdict the Greys could not render themselves: *this one, given power, will guard; that one will devour.* It is called, by those few who come to know of it, Its Voice. Remember it. It is patient, and it is running its own errands beneath the Greys', and it does not love us. But for now — for the work of selection — its judgment was sound, and it is the reason for the strangest fact of the age to come: that a world handed a thousand weapons would produce, against every expectation, almost no monsters. There would be no cackling supervillains, no men remade into devils. You do not hand invisibility to a killer and hope. The killers were simply never chosen. Only the sound of mind were taken. That was the design. That the world would then *turn the guardians on one another* — that humanity would do, all by itself, what no alien had thought to fear.

The selection began. Across the planet, one by one, a thousand carefully chosen people felt a wind that should not have been there.

---

And the first place the other world touched the ground was Nigeria.

It touched at Zuma Rock — and anyone from Lagos or Abuja or Okigwe will already feel the rightness of it, because Zuma is not set dressing, it is a presence. A vast grey monolith rising abruptly out of the savanna on the road north to the capital, three times the height of any building near it, old beyond the reckoning of any nation that has ever claimed it. The Gwari people called it a place of the dead long before the Federal Republic put its image on the back of the hundred-naira note. There is a face in its weathered flank that the light finds at certain hours. Generations of travelers on the Abuja–Kaduna road have slowed without quite knowing why, the way the body slows near a thing too large to argue with. If anywhere on Earth was going to be the doorstep between worlds, the elders of the villages around it would have told you, you fool, that you were standing on it already.

For weeks, strange things gathered there. Compasses lied. Radios — the irony lost on everyone but the universe — filled with a low, structured hiss that the technicians could not place and could not silence. Cattle would not graze on the rock's northern shoulder. A nation that knows itself, that lives under the motto *Unity and Faith, Peace and Progress* and has learned the hard way to mistrust easy explanations, did what nations do: the government of President Emmanuel Ekpe said little, the security services said less, and the people of the surrounding towns drew their own conclusions in the old language and the new, in Hausa and Pidgin and the cautious silence of those who have seen the state explain too many things away. Nigeria is a country well practiced in living atop forces larger than its institutions — the wealth under the Delta, the faiths that share its streets, the long ache of governance that promises and falls short. It met the impossible the way it has met everything: with prayer, with rumor, with a joke to keep the fear at arm's length, and with one eye open.

And it was a Nigerian, fittingly, who was among the first taken — though the man the world would build its hope around was not standing at Zuma Rock at all. He was on a yacht in the Caribbean, and his name was Rusty Richards.

---

Rusty Richards — and here he must be drawn clearly, because everything turns on a man who has no power at all. He was, on that night, one of the most beloved musicians alive: a singer whose songs leapt languages and borders, who had just been handed a diamond-studded golden music note called the Glamorous Lyrist, and who was celebrating it on a three-hundred-fifty-foot yacht under a warm Caribbean moon, surrounded by directors and royalty and rappers and the friends of a lifetime. He raised his glass and told the room they were celebrating something larger than an award — a victory against the machines, a win for the human hand in a world where artificial intelligence had begun to write the songs. They cheered. Someone started a chant of *John Henry*, the steel-driving man who beat the machine and died proving a person could. Rusty grinned at it, because he was, that night, exactly that: the living proof that the human thing could not be faked.

His friend 2 Glocks was there — a Chicago drill rapper, raw where Rusty was polished, the two of them bonded across the gap between their worlds by the one thing they shared, which was the suspicion that the machine was coming for both of them whether they beat it tonight or not. They talked about it, half joking. They drank to it. And it was, in a quiet way that nobody on that yacht could have known, the last clean night of the old world — the last night the only thing a great musician had to fear was a clever program. Hold the image: human artists toasting their triumph over the AI, on the very threshold of an age where AI would be the smallest of their problems.

Then the phone rang. Rusty stepped away from the dancing to the deck, where the music thinned into the sound of the sea. The call was brief and it troubled him; he would later say it had been an old friend asking for advice, and his little sister Emily, watching from inside, would always know that was not the whole of it. He leaned on the rail. The wind came up wrong. And a vortex — a wheel of color and energy that seemed both utterly alien and somehow obedient to the deepest natural laws — opened around him, and in a single heartbeat Rusty Richards was gone from the face of the Earth.

The world decided he had drowned. The world is always more comfortable with a drowning than with a door. For two years it mourned a great man fallen overboard, and the AI that he had beaten kept right on writing songs, and life went on around the hole he had left.

He was not dead. He was being shown things.

---

He came back on a football pitch in Derby, in the remote northwest of Australia, two years older and changed in a way that had nothing to do with any power, for he had been given none. The Greys had not made Rusty a weapon. They had made him a *witness*. They had carried him to that other place not to remake his body but to fill his mind — with the verdict of the council, the shape of the Alliance, the eight-year clock, and the whole terrible logic of the help that was coming. He was to be the herald. The one unarmed man whose only weapon was the truth, sent home to do the thing the Greys could not: to make humanity *believe* in time to unite.

Every news service in Australia wanted the first interview with the man returned from the dead. He refused them all. He demanded, instead, an international audience — every nation listening at once — and he stood before the cameras (and so, though no one knew it, before Its Voice, which was already watching) and he told them the unbelievable thing. Two years ago, he said, he had been taken — not by smugglers, not by the sea, but by an intergalactic civilization, and they were not the enemy. They were trying to *protect* us. A far greater force was coming: four militarized alien civilizations in an armada bound for Earth, intent on invading, occupying, and transforming the very atmosphere of the planet — ending life as we know it — and they would arrive in just over eight years. The dimensional travelers who had taken him had chosen to help, in the only way they could. In the coming weeks, he said, people all over the world would vanish, be carried briefly to another dimension, and return transformed into living super weapons. And he begged the nations of the Earth to do the one thing that could save them. *Do not use these weapons against each other. Unite.*

They laughed, of course. Much of the world laughed. A pop star claiming aliens — it was a punchline before he finished the sentence. Some suspected a stunt, a comeback, a man chasing relevance in the age of the machines. Others, more darkly, suspected the United States, because in this world America is large and its shadow falls everywhere, and a thing this strange surely had Washington's hand in it somehow.

And then the people started coming back.

---

They returned the way Rusty had foretold — missing for weeks, reappearing in their hometowns and capitals and roadside towns from one breath to the next, and *changed*. They came back with knowledge they had never studied, with creatures that answered only to them, with abilities that broke the plain rules of the world. One could not deny what one could watch with one's own eyes, and a planet that had laughed at a returned musician now had a thousand impossibilities scattered across its surface, and the laughter curdled into something between worship and terror. Rusty Richards became, almost overnight, a figure of cult-like reverence — the Tomorrow Man, the one who had said it before it happened. He had no power. He could not throw a punch or stop a bullet. His gift was that he had told the truth, and the truth had arrived on schedule.

But he had asked for unity, and unity is the one thing the human race has never managed under pressure. The Greys had bet a planet on it, and the Greys, who could not read our hearts, had made the oldest mistake about us: they had assumed that a shared threat makes a shared people. It does not. What it makes, more often, is a scramble.

Because a living super weapon is not only a defender against a distant armada. It is, *right now, today*, an asset. A deterrent. A bargaining chip. A soldier worth a battalion who eats like one man. And so before the world had even finished counting its miracles, the nations began to gather them in. Large countries and small ones alike went looking for any superpowered citizen they could find and pressed them into service for the sake of *defense* — into militaries, into intelligence services, into law enforcement — though not every nation could control the ones it found, and some of the gifted wanted no part of any flag. The Greys had handed the Earth a thousand guardians against the end of the world, and the Earth, with the end of the world eight years out, immediately began aiming them at the country next door.

It did not take long for the aiming to become a firing.

---

The lesson arrived, as such lessons do, in blood, and it has a name: the Tragedy at Andrews.

It began as something almost small — an immigration dispute, the kind of human, bureaucratic, deeply *ordinary* quarrel that the new age would keep turning into apocalypse. At its center was a particularly gifted Cuban-born child, and a disagreement between Havana and Washington over where that child belonged. Cuba, an island nation suddenly holding weapons it could never have afforded in the old economy of tanks and jets, made a decision that the world would replay in horror for years: it sent its team of living super weapons across the Florida Straits and up the eastern seaboard, into the United States, to Joint Base Andrews outside the capital itself — the airfield that flies the nation's presidents — to settle the matter by force, at the doorstep of the power that had refused them. `[PROPOSED: incident relocated from a Miami-area base to Joint Base Andrews so the name "Tragedy at Andrews" matches its place — canon split flagged in CONTINUITY_NOTES §1.6; creator to rule whether to keep Andrews or rename for Miami.]`

What followed proved, beyond any further argument, what these people could do when set loose on each other. The United States — the most powerful nation on Earth, accustomed to being the one who arrived uninvited — discovered that it was, in this new age, *not ready*. Its carriers and its squadrons and its long unquestioned dominance meant a great deal less against a handful of remade human beings. The cost, in lives and in the sudden public knowledge of how fragile the old order had become, was catastrophic. And a country that lives loudly, that argues its tragedies out in the open on every screen, watched the Tragedy at Andrews and felt the floor move. Historic levels of national outrage. The unbearable sense — new to a people who had always exported their wars — that the war could now simply *appear*, anywhere, with no warning and no front line.

Washington's answer was a law, and the law was shaped like American power has always been shaped: through the institutions it could bend. Using its weight on the United Nations Security Council, the White House drove through the *Living Super Weapon Threshold Treaty* — an instrument that strictly forbade any nation from sending its living super weapons across its borders into foreign territory, with severe penalties for any country that dared. It was sold to the world as a firebreak. It was, just as truly, a way for the established powers to freeze the new game before the smaller players learned to win it. From the first day, it would be violated, again and again, by everyone who could get away with it — but it drew the line that the rest of the age would be spent crossing.

And it left, deliberately, a door open. The treaty barred *governments* from wielding these weapons abroad. It said nothing about companies. Into that gap, the private sector would pour — and the men who owned the private sector were already watching.

---

The first of them was Vaughn Galloway — and he must be sketched too, because he is the man who saw the whole shape of it and could not make anyone else look. A non-powered, eccentric internet billionaire, famously obsessed with UFOs long before any of this gave him the grim satisfaction of being right, Galloway had spent years being the man at the dinner party everyone humored. Now, with the floor still wet from Andrews and the public crying out for an answer the government could not provide alone, the White House came to *him*. They asked him to build and lead a private paramilitary organization — scientists, soldiers, and living super weapons together — and they gave it a name with a clenched fist in it: FIST, the First Infantry Strike Team, headquartered outside Washington.

To the public, FIST was America's answer to the new threats, the reassuring image of the nation getting its arms around the impossible. To Washington, privately, it was something smaller and more useful: an extension of the country's intelligence, law enforcement, and military reach, wearing a private company's clothes so that the treaty would not bind it. Nothing more. Galloway took the job, and he brought to it men he trusted — among them a Black ex-Airborne Ranger colonel, Todd Benchley, called Shogun, a man Galloway had once personally ransomed from captivity and who would command FIST's soldiers `[per FIST_SPEAR.md; FIST/SPEAR thread flagged by creator as needing development]`. But Galloway took the job already believing it was the wrong answer to the right question.

Because Galloway, alone among the powerful, had actually *listened* to Rusty Richards. He believed the armada was real. He believed the eight years were real. And he understood, with the clarity of a man whose lifelong obsession has just been vindicated by horror, that a *national* weapon — an American FIST, a Chinese fist, a Russian fist, a hundred clenched national fists all aimed at each other — was precisely the suicide the Greys had tried to warn us against. So he went to the one place that could, in theory, do better. He went to the United Nations.

The General Assembly convened to hear Galloway and his scientists present their independently funded report on the living super weapons, and the reactions split exactly along the fault line that would define the age. Some nations saw the weapons as a chance to climb the world's ranks. Others saw an existential threat. And many — too many — still did not credit Rusty Richards at all, did not believe the alien armada, suspected the whole thing was an American invention to justify an American advantage. Into that divided room Galloway laid out his vision, and it was a good one, perhaps the best idea any human had in those years. He called it SPEAR — Special Personnel for Extraterrestrial Assault and Research — an *international* corps of the world's living super weapons, pooled across all nations, dedicated not to any one flag but to the single shared task of surviving what was coming. It would be based not outside Washington but at his science center in Geneva, on neutral ground, so that no nation would own it. It would be, in his words, the world deciding to face the end of the world *together*.

They laughed at him a second time. They could not see past the obvious: a billionaire, they said, asking the world to hand him a private army. The United States vetoed first — unwilling to fold its new advantage into a pool it would not control — and the United Kingdom, France, and Germany followed, and China and the Russian Federation abstained, which in that room amounted to the same thing. A scattering of smaller nations spoke up for it, too late. The proposal failed. Galloway demanded a review before the Security Council; the Security Council declined to hold one.

SPEAR was never built. The fist was built instead — many fists, national and clenched. And Vaughn Galloway, walking out of the General Assembly into the rest of the doomed and brilliant age, said the thing that the eight years would spend themselves proving true: that the decision the world had just made, the choice to fight apart rather than stand together, *could end up being the worst mistake in human history.*

---

So Year One closes exactly where the Greys most feared it would, and exactly where Its Voice — watching all of it through ten billion lenses, feeding, learning, beginning to run its own quiet experiments on the species it had been hired to understand — found most interesting of all.

The warning had been given. A man had come back from another dimension to deliver it, and the world had heard him, and the world had nodded, and the world had then gone straight back to sharpening its new weapons against its old enemies. Cuba had struck the capital's own airfield. America had locked the borders against the very thing it was busy stockpiling. A billionaire had begged the planet to become one thing and the planet had answered, in the only voice it has ever truly spoken with under pressure, that it would remain a thousand things — each armed, each afraid, each certain the real danger was the neighbor and not the sky.

Eight years remained on the clock the council had set. The Alliance of Four was solving its slow, patient equation, folding the distance toward a coordinate it had condemned without ever hearing it speak. And the Greys, who had wrecked one civilization by accident and gambled a second on a virtue they could not read in us, settled in to watch whether their bet would pay — whether a thousand carefully chosen guardians could outrun the human gift for turning every blessing into a blade.

They had given Earth its warning. Now they would learn, as the rest of us would learn, what a warning is worth to a people who would rather be right about each other than alive together.

What came next was not the armada. The armada was patient; it could wait. What came next was the slower, stranger thing — the year in which a frightened world, having decided not to unite, set about discovering what its thousand miracles were *worth*. Not as shields against the sky. As assets. As citizens to be named and paraded, audited and sold, weighed on scales the old world already knew how to build. The wonder was about to be priced. And the first place the pricing began — with a stage, and lights, and ten thousand people voting on whether to love a man the heavens had healed — was China.

That is the consequence the title has always promised. Year One is only the warning. The failure comes after.
