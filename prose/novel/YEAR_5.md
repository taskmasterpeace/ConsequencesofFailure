# YEAR 5 — "THE DYING"

*Consequences of Failure — the novel, first draft. Third-person omniscient; narrative summary; speech in indirect / free-indirect discourse. Built to be read aloud.*

---

There is a number that the living were never meant to know, and in the fifth year the world learned it anyway.

It began, as the great dyings always begin, not with the dying but with a clerk. A woman in a records office in Bogotá, three floors above a street where the trolleybuses had stopped running because the Iron Eaters had gotten into the overhead lines, sat at a desk and entered death certificates into a spreadsheet, the way she had every working day for eleven years. By March she had stopped reading the names. By April she had stopped entering them one at a time and had begun entering them in blocks, fifty rows pasted at once, the cause-of-death column filled down with a single keystroke. By the morning she did not come in, the spreadsheet had grown too large to open, and no one came to fix it, and that — a file too heavy to lift, in a building with no power, in a city that had begun to empty — was as good a monument as the year would get. The number was never finished. That was the truth of it. Half the world is not a figure you arrive at. It is a figure you stop being able to count.

But to understand how it came to ordinary people — to the clerk, to her neighbors, to the four billion who had no power, no gift, no place in any of it — you have to go back to the thing in the South American soil, and to the smaller, quieter horror the previous year had buried under the louder one.

---

The monster-virus had been, until now, a weapon that respected its own caste. It had been born the year before out of a genetic experiment gone catastrophically wrong — two superpowered people, a laboratory that thought it understood what it was splicing, and a result no one had meant to make: a pathogen that turned the gifted into monsters. That was its grim mercy, if a plague can be said to have one. It fed on what made a Living Super Weapon a Living Super Weapon. Whatever the alien gifting had done to those few thousand bodies — rewired the cells, woke the dormant code, made flesh do what flesh does not do — the virus needed exactly that altered machinery to live. An ordinary man it could enter and not use. It would sit in his blood like a guest who finds no door he can open, and after a few days his immune system would show it out, and he would have a fever and a bad week and live. So for a year the dying had been contained inside the smallest population on Earth. Tragic, and bounded. The world had grieved its monsters and felt, in the cowardly private way of the spared, safe.

The insight the world's virologists kept circling, in the months before it stopped mattering, was simple and terrible. A virus does not *want* anything. It has no plan to jump from the gifted to the rest. But a virus is a copying machine running a billion errors a second, and a copying machine that large, left running long enough in enough bodies, will eventually copy itself *wrong* in exactly the way that opens a new door. The gifted were a small population — but the virus inside each of them was an ocean, and the gifted kept bleeding, and their blood was harvested and trafficked by the Jackals and the fluid-economy that had grown up around them, and so the virus got to run its billion-errors-a-second experiment in places no laboratory could watch. It needed only one variant, in one body, that no longer required the altered machinery. One key cut by accident that fit the commonest lock in the world.

It was found, when it was finally traced, to have happened somewhere in the trafficking chain itself — in a cold-storage drum of pooled, illegally drawn LSW plasma, exactly the kind of thing the Treaty had failed to stop and the Jackals had built an empire on. A variant that could enter an ordinary cell and turn it the way it turned a gifted one. The door was open. And on the far side of the door was everyone.

---

Stampede heard about it the way most people did, from a screen, standing in a kitchen that was not his.

John Rivers — ex-LAPD, the gravity in his hands that could pin a man to the ground or fling a car off an overpass, the brother who had torn the Shooter's Club apart over the murder of his sister Rebecca Carranza — had spent the years since becoming the most wanted unpowered-turned-powered fugitive in America, which is to say he had spent them in other people's kitchens. He was watching a wall-mounted television in a safe apartment in El Paso when the first ordinary-person case in the United States was confirmed, and he understood, before the anchor finished the sentence, that the country that had been hunting him was about to have very much larger problems, and that this would not, in the way he might once have hoped, make them forget about him. It would do the opposite. A frightened nation looks for a face.

But that is later. First the world simply came apart, and it came apart unevenly, the way everything in this world had always come apart — along the old fault lines, the rich high and the poor low, so that the plague did to the map exactly what the map had spent centuries doing to people, only faster, and with no pretense.

---

In the wealthy north the first weeks looked almost like competence. There were stockpiles. There were ventilators, though the Iron Eaters had eaten a great many of them in the previous year, devouring the metal of the machines that the sick now needed. There were governments that could, for a while, pay people to stay home. The television showed orderly lines and field hospitals in convention centers and a great deal of language about flattening and curves and the heroism of the exhausted. For about a month the wealthy nations believed, as the spared always believe, that money was a kind of antibody.

Then the supply chains, already gnawed half through by the metal-blight, simply stopped, because a supply chain is people, and the people were sick or afraid or dead, and a truck does not drive itself across a continent for a country that has run out of the will to make it. The orderly lines became something else. There is a particular sound a society makes when it discovers that its order was a habit and not a law, and in the fifth year that sound went up over every city on Earth, the rich ones only a few weeks behind the poor.

In the poor south there had never been the month of competence. There the dying skipped the prologue. In the great informal cities — the ones the Country Master Sheet listed with their hopeful mottos and their quantified corruption, the ones where half a nation might live stacked in a single sprawl with one clinic between a hundred thousand souls — the virus moved at the speed of breath, and there were no ventilators to eat and no stockpiles to ration and no government that had ever been trusted enough to be obeyed. What there was, instead, was each other. The thing the world's comfortable had spent the plague rediscovering — that you survive a dying not through your institutions but through the person next to you — the poor had never had the luxury of forgetting. So in the places that buried the most, you also saw, against all the arithmetic, the most carrying: the neighbor who took in the orphaned, the market woman who cooked for a street that could no longer cook for itself, the boy who ran water to the houses where everyone was too weak to walk to the tap. It did not save them. Most of it did not save anyone. But it meant that even at the bottom of the year, the species was still, in its small stubborn cells, behaving like something worth saving, and that mattered later, to the ones who had to decide whether it was.

---

Two places, out of the hundred and seventy the storyteller could choose from, will stand here for the rest — because to say *half the world* is to say a number, and a number is a place the listener's heart goes to die. Better to say India, and then to say Bangladesh, and let two specific grounds carry the weight the abstraction cannot.

India went into the fifth year the largest population on Earth — one nation in seven of all the living, more than a billion and a third souls, a federal republic whose motto, carved over its institutions, read *Truth alone triumphs.* It was a country that had spent the gifted years better than most, because it had understood early what the gift was for. Establishment 24, the body the future-traveler Asha and the golden-armored Colonel Reddy had built to rebuild a nation, had already done a thing no one else had managed — it had cleaned and remade the Ganga, the river a few hundred million people drank from and burned their dead beside and called mother, in a feat of engineered restoration the country had named the *Icchatām Ganga*, the Ganga of will, the river made what people wished it to be. That was the cruelty of what came next. The plague reached India along the same rivers and the same rails and the same dense braided closeness that had always been the country's genius and its exposure, and the *Icchatām Ganga* — clean now, beautiful, restored — carried the dead of the fifth year down through Varanasi and Patna and Kolkata in numbers the burning-ghats could not keep pace with, so that a river engineered to be the symbol of a nation's recovery became the largest moving grave in human history. The ghats burned around the clock. When the wood ran out — and the wood ran out — the families did the older thing, the thing the river had always been asked to do, and gave their dead to the water, and the *Icchatām Ganga*, the river of will, was asked at last to carry a grief no one had willed at all.

Asha lived the year worst of anyone who lived it twice. The future-traveler's gift had always cost her in the only currency a mind keeps — she went forward and came back carrying fragments she could not hold, paying for each glimpse in pieces of her own memory, so that a woman who could see tomorrow could not always remember yesterday. In a plague year that gift became a particular cruelty. She went forward and saw the count that could not be finished, the river of the dead, the ghats that ran out of wood, and she came back unable to keep most of it and unable to forget the worst of it, spending her own self to bring home warnings that arrived too late or too broken to act on. By autumn the people of Establishment 24 had learned to read her the way one reads weather — not for the details she could never retain, but for the look she wore when she returned, which told them only whether the thing ahead was survivable. It was not, that year, a power that saved anyone. It was a power that grieved in advance, and paid for the privilege.

And here the country's character showed, the way character shows only under a load that would break it. India did not, in the main, abandon its dead. A civilization that had spent five thousand years building elaborate, exact, loving rites for the passage out of life does not become, even at near-to-half, a civilization that lets its people go unmarked. So the priests went on, hoarse and dropping, performing the rites in batches and then in throngs and then for the anonymous floating dead they would never know the names of, because the rite was owed to the soul and not to the paperwork, and a Brahmin in Varanasi who has buried his own family does not stop chanting for strangers merely because the strangers have become too many to count. The state buckled — President Tulsidas Narayan's government, like every government that year, was revealed to be a habit more than a law — but the country did not, because the country had never really been the state. It had been the rite, and the river, and the unbearable crowded human closeness, and those held even as the bodies came down the holy water by the hundred thousand. The insight a watching alien might have drawn, and did: that the same density which made India the plague's richest feeding ground also made it the hardest place on Earth to convince that a person could be allowed to die alone. The crush that killed them was the crush that would not let them be forgotten.

Downriver, where the Ganga and the Brahmaputra braid together and spill into the sea, was Bangladesh, and Bangladesh is where the fifth year wrote its plainest sentence about who pays. A hundred and sixty million people on a delta the size of a single mid-sized country elsewhere — the most crowded large nation on Earth, a parliamentary democracy whose motto, *Joy Bangla*, victory to Bengal, had been won at a cost the country never let itself forget. It went into the plague with a healthcare system the surveys rated barely above failing and a geography that was, even in a good year, a slow argument with the water — a land that floods by season and design, where a third of the country can go under in a hard monsoon and come back, where the people had made an entire civilization out of the genius of living on ground that does not stay still. That genius had a dark twin in a plague. You cannot distance, on a delta. There is no spreading out where there is no out to spread to; the land is full to its edges and the edges are water. And so the virus, which is only ever a thing that travels between breathing bodies, found in the Bengal delta the closest-packed breathing bodies in the world, and it did there what it did, and the rate of it outran every model the dying virologists could still bother to run.

But the second thing Bangladesh did is the thing worth carrying out of the year, and it is the thing the Country Master Sheet's bloodless columns — corruption, healthcare, perception — could never have predicted, because it does not live in columns. A delta people are flood people, and flood people have a thousand-year-old technology the rich north had let itself forget: they know how to be carried, and how to carry. When the water comes, on the delta, you do not wait for the state; the state is a boat that arrives next week. You put the children and the elders on the roof, and the strong wade, and the neighbor with the boat makes the trip he does not have to make, again and again, until the boat or the neighbor gives out. They turned that on the plague. The same networks of obligation that moved a village to high ground in a flood — the same boats, the same rooftops, the same unwritten law that you do not let the next family drown merely because it is not yours — became, in the fifth year, the delivery system that kept a doomed country alive in numbers it had no statistical right to keep. When the cure finally came, months too late, it did not come down a supply chain, because there was no supply chain; it came the way everything in the delta has always come, hand to boat to rooftop to hand, and it reached places no truck could ever have reached because the people moving it had been moving each other through rising water their whole lives. Bangladesh buried a horror. Bangladesh also proved, more clearly than the wealthy north ever managed, that the thing that saves a people in a dying is not its budget. It is its practice of one another.

---

The toll climbed. It is the historian's job to give a dying its proportions, and the proportion of the fifth year was this: that it did not fall on a class or a continent but on the whole of the living at once, and that by the time it crested it had taken something near to one in two. Not evenly. Never evenly. But everywhere. There was no nation that did not have its emptied streets, its silent schools, its trucks that came in the night because the daytime could no longer bear to watch them. Whole governments fell — not overthrown, simply *vacated*, the way a house is vacated, the ministers dead or fled or barricaded and irrelevant. The seat of more than one country became, for a season, a single overwhelmed official broadcasting from a basement to a population that had stopped listening because it had stopped being.

And here is the thing the survivors would argue about for the rest of their lives: that even at the worst of it, the world did not entirely become a monster. It became a thing in two minds. For every district that sealed its borders and shot the refugees at the line, there was a district that opened. For every man who hoarded, a man who shared. The plague was a great revealer, and what it revealed was not one verdict on humanity but the oldest argument humanity has ever had with itself, conducted now at the scale of the entire species and with the stakes set, for once, all the way to the top. The aliens, watching, would later find this the single most interesting datum of the eight years — not that humanity died, but that it could not, even dying, agree on what kind of thing it wished to be.

---

It was into the very pit of that argument that the Sages walked, and it is here that the year turns from a chronicle of dying into something stranger, because the year that buried half the world is also the year humanity did the most impossible thing it ever did.

To understand what the Sages spent that year, you have to understand what a Sage is, and the world still did not entirely know — that part was a mystery that would not unravel for a long while yet. What the world saw was only the surface miracle: that scattered across the Earth were a few people who had woken one ordinary morning with the *complete* knowledge of a single discipline poured into them whole, as though they had spent four lifetimes mastering one subject and not one hour of it doing the work. A woman who could not boil an egg but who held, perfect and entire, the whole of organic chemistry. A man who had never set foot in a hospital who carried the sum of clinical medicine behind his eyes and wept, the first time they put him in a ward, because his hands did not know what his mind already understood. That was the cruelty and the wonder of a Sage in one: godlike knowing, infant doing. A Sage knew everything and had never done anything, and so a Sage was the most powerful and the most helpless person in any room, useless without a lab, a hospital, a people, a *world* to point them at — and now, at last, the world pointed.

They did not work alone, and they could not have. The cure for the fifth-year plague was not the achievement of one luminous mind. It was the achievement of every surviving mind that could be wired to a luminous one — and that, by the strange mercy of the gifting, was possible. In Armenia, Karine Abrahamian, the abducted student remade into a node of NuroNuro brain-sharing, became, in the worst months, a kind of switchboard for the human intellect: a way for a virology Sage in one ruined city to think *with* a genetics Sage in another, the two of them sharing a single working mind across a thousand kilometers while the bodies that housed them shook with fever and grief. What had been built as a weapon, or a curiosity, or a violation, became the nervous system of the species' last good idea.

It is worth pausing on what that cost the woman who was the switchboard, because the year took its prices from the ones who could least be spared, and Karine paid in the currency of self. The thing she did was not telephony; it was not passing messages between minds that stayed their own. To wire two Sages together so they could hold a single problem between them, she had to *be* the wire — to open herself and let two foreign intellects run their work through the channel of her own head, the way current runs through copper and warms it and, run hard enough and long enough, melts it. A virologist's terror and a geneticist's stubbornness and the sheer cliff-face of a problem neither could solve alone all crossed through Karine Abrahamian's skull, hour after hour, and some of it stayed, the way the dead stayed in Kaiser. By autumn she could no longer reliably find the border of which thoughts were hers. She would reach for an Armenian word from her abducted girlhood and come up holding a stranger's equation. The survivors who later told her story were careful to say that she never once asked to be unplugged, and careful, too, to say that this was not heroism in the clean sense, but something stranger and harder — a person who had been made into an instrument deciding, while she still had enough of herself left to decide anything, that being an instrument for *this* was the first thing that had ever been done to her that she would have chosen.

But the spine of it — the place the cure was actually wrung out of the dark — was Uganda, and a Sage the world had filed under the wrong name and the wrong story. Cissy Oliva, genetic-engineering Sage, the richest prisoner in Africa, the woman whose godlike knowledge of the gene had until now been spent making custom animals for men with more money than conscience, found herself for the first time pointed at the only problem worth her knowing.

The world had treated Cissy as a wonder to be owned. She had built, under guard, an empire of designed creatures; she had been the goose and the golden egg both, and never once free. And now the same powers that had caged her came to her with the plague in their own blood and asked her, in effect, to be the thing they had refused to let her be: a person. She could have refused. She had every reason on Earth to refuse. The grim, illuminating fact the survivors would remember was that she did not, and that she did not because of the carrying she had seen — because even her captors, dying, were still bringing each other water — and she decided that a species that did that, even at the bottom, was a species she would not let go down on her watch.

What a Sage's cure actually required is worth saying plainly, because it is the heart of the year and it was not magic, only the most brutal kind of work. To cure the plague Cissy did not need to invent anything. She *knew* the answer the way she knew her own name; the knowing was the whole of her gift. What she needed was the doing, and the doing of genetics is not thought but time and matter and bodies. It is growing the right cells in the right medium and watching most of them die. It is the same experiment run ten thousand times with one variable changed, because the cell does not care what you know, only what you do to it, and it tells you nothing back but yes or no, slowly. The variant that had opened the door to ordinary people had opened it through a single change, one cut key — and the cure was, in essence, a key cut to jam the lock: a tailored fragment, harmless in itself, that taught the body to recognize the one new shape the virus wore and to shut the door the virus had pried open. Cissy could have drawn the design in an afternoon. Making it real, and making enough of it to matter, and getting it into arms across a world whose roads the Iron Eaters had eaten — that took the year, and it took the most of everyone who spent it, and it came too late for most of the people it was meant to save.

It is the second half of that — the *making it real* — that the world, in its grief, mostly failed to honor, because it is not a heroic story. It is a story of a brilliant woman watching things die in trays. The body has a defense against learning the wrong lesson, and the cure had to teach the right lesson without tripping that defense, and the line between *teaches the body to fight the virus* and *teaches the body to fight itself* is, in a discipline as exact as genetics, a single hydrogen bond wide. Cissy crossed it wrong, by her own later count, more than four hundred times. Each crossing was not an abstraction; it was a batch grown over days that turned, at the end, lethal or inert, and had to be incinerated, and begun again, while outside the sealed rooms the trucks she could hear but not see went on coming. She had no animals to test on that could stand in for a human immune system altered by an alien gift, because the alien gift did not exist in any animal — so the testing, at the end, when the design was as close as cell-trays could bring it, had to be done on the only suitable subjects there were, which were dying people, who volunteered, because they were dying anyway and because a delta people and a river people and a continent of carriers had taught the whole species, that year, what one does with a body one is about to lose. The names of the early volunteers, the ones the not-yet-finished cure killed faster than the plague would have, are not in any history, and Cissy Oliva, who never met them, learned every one she could and said them to herself, the way a riverside priest says the rite over the unnamed floating dead, because someone has to hold the ones the saving costs.

There was a night — the survivors who reconstructed the year placed it in the deep of the worst winter — when Cissy had the cure and could not prove she had it, when the design on the screen matched the design in her gift in every line and the cell-trays would not yet say yes, and she sat in a sealed Ugandan room with Karine Abrahamian's borrowed minds gone quiet from exhaustion on the far end of the wire, and did the arithmetic of it: that every day she spent confirming what she already *knew* was a day measured in a city's worth of the dead, and that to release it unconfirmed was to risk killing the survivors she was trying to save, and that there was no answer to that equation, only a choice, and the choice was hers and no one else's because she was the only person on Earth who could read both sides of it. That is the costliest hour the storyteller can offer, and it is not a battle. It is a woman alone with a number, choosing how many strangers to gamble against how many strangers, knowing whichever way she chose the dead would be real and the choice would be hers to carry. She waited four more days. The four days have a price written against them in lives, and so does every one of the days she did not wait. There was no version without a price. That was the year entire, set in one sealed room.

And then, when at last the trays said yes, there was the final cruelty, which was that a cure is only a piece of paper until it is in an arm, and the arms were scattered across a planet whose roads the Iron Eaters had eaten and whose ports had stopped and whose order had been revealed to be a habit. Cissy Oliva solved the plague in a sealed room in Uganda; the plague went on killing for months afterward in places the solution could not physically reach, and a great many of the fifth year's last dead died not of an unsolved disease but of distance — of being on the wrong side of a broken road from a cure that already existed. It was here that everything else in the year turned out to have been, secretly, preparation. Kaiser's continent moved it along the network of mutual aid his dream had built; in Tanzania the coup that had begun the year before finished its slow turn, and the silent man who had won it — Jawah Matu, who had taken a country by absorbing every sound that came at him until the men with guns ran out of anything to do but stop — used the quiet he had bought to run the cure up-country through villages that had learned, in his rising, to trust the man who never raised his voice; Johnny Rain sang it into towns that would have shot a government truck and trusted a song; the Bengal delta carried it hand to boat to rooftop; the Indian rites went on long enough to keep a people coherent until it arrived. The cure was a Sage's. The *delivery* of it was the whole carrying species, every neighbor and boatman and hoarse singer and chanting priest, doing across a planet the exact thing the poor had been doing all year on their own streets. The knowledge came from one caged woman's gift. Reaching the dying with it took everyone who had refused, that year, to let the person next to them go down alone.

That was the cost, and it must be set down without flinching, because the Sages themselves never let themselves forget it. The cure worked. The cure also came after the dying had already taken its near-to-half. Every day Cissy Oliva and her switchboard of borrowed minds and exhausted hands shaved off the front end of that timeline, thousands lived who would otherwise have died — and every day it could not be shaved was a day the trucks still came. The finest hour of the human intellect was also its costliest, and the people who achieved it did not celebrate when it was done. They had spent the year inside the gap between *knowing* the answer and *being in time*, and that gap, they understood, was the whole tragedy of the species set down in miniature: humanity had never lacked the knowledge to save itself. It had only ever lacked the time, and the time was always spent on something else.

---

And what humanity spent its time on, while the Sages worked, was killing the one man who had spent his telling them they could do better.

Kaiser Eziobi — the Lagos helicopter pilot remade into the Memory Vessel, the man with an alien slug curled behind his eyes and a million African dead circulating in his blood, the man who had stood at the rostrum of the United Nations and told the nations of the Earth that Africa was not seeking their permission — entered the fifth year at the height of a power no one had thought possible. The dream had cleared its last horizon. The pieces of the African Union that had laughed at his five promises had, vote by vote and year by year, stopped laughing, and the dying was, monstrously, *helping* him. Because while the wealthy north discovered that money was not an antibody, Kaiser's continent discovered that the thing he had been building all along — the roads to every village, the shield over all of it, the fifty-five states learning at last to act as one body — was precisely the machinery a plague is fought with. Where Kaiser's movement had reached, the carrying was organized. Aïssata's hands found the water that kept the camps alive. Johnny Rain, hoarse and unbought, turned his fame into a logistics network no government could have bought and sang the cure into towns that would have trusted no official who brought it. And Faud — the reformed Akrahuhum leader who had once put a rifle to Kaiser's head and now stood between him and every room full of strangers — ran the security of a continent's mutual aid like a man working off a debt he knew he could never finish.

It worked. That was the unforgivable thing, from a certain point of view. Kaiser's Africa, the poorest ground on Earth, was for one impossible season *holding* — buckling and bleeding like everywhere else, but holding together, governed in the plague better than the rich north was governing itself, and demonstrating to four billion frightened people that the old order was not the only order, that a continent the world had spent five hundred years treating as a quarry could stand on its own feet in the worst hour in human history and not fall. It was the most dangerous thing in the world. Not the plague. The proof.

The decision to kill him was never, of course, taken in a room with a name on the door. It surfaced the way such decisions always surface in this world — in the bland murderous language of unsigned papers, in the alignment of interests that need never be spoken aloud to be agreed. The pretext, when they reached for one, was Stampede. Kaiser had defended John Rivers. He had said, in plain words, what the Memory Vessel made it impossible for him not to say: that a man hunted across a nation for tearing down a unit of murderers was not the villain in that story, that the powers calling Stampede a monster were the same kind of powers, finally, as the ones who had built the rocket that fell his helicopter on a dirt road outside Damboa — people for whom a man was a quarry and his dying a cost of business. Kaiser had carried a million of those dying. He could not unsay it. And it gave the interests that already wanted him gone the only thing they had lacked, which was a sentence to point at.

He knew it was coming. He had known since the night before the UN, when the unsigned page had arrived on the desk in the Manhattan hotel and Faud had said *they're frightened* and Kaiser had said *good*. A man who has grieved his own death from the inside out, who has died a hundred clinic deaths borrowing the last moments of the drowned and the fevered and the alone, is very hard to threaten with the thing itself. They had not been able to make him afraid. So they made him dead.

---

How it was done does not deserve more dignity than it had. It was done at a distance, by people who never saw his face, in the manner the powerful have always reserved for the inconveniently good — not the rifle in the room but the order down the wire, deniable, regrettable, *destabilizing-element-neutralized*. Faud's body had stood between Kaiser and the strangers for years; Faud's body could not stand between Kaiser and a decision made in a building on another continent by men who had decided that the proof was more dangerous than the plague.

What deserves the telling is not the killing but the slug, and the dead, and what they did.

Because Kaiser Eziobi did not die the way a man dies. When Life Form 0 understood that the body it had shared for a decade was ending — when the warm patient presence at the back of his skull felt the rooms of its long-lived house begin to go dark — it did the thing it had been doing, in its blind alien way, since a fisherman drowned off Lagos in 1978. It kept what was passing. The man who had spent his remade life being the only one who would not look away from the unseen dead became, in his last minutes, one of them, and the creature gathered him in the way it had gathered all the others, soldering the feeling to the image so that whoever held him next would not be able to have the one without the other. Kaiser Eziobi went into the vessel he had been. The keeper became the kept.

And the slug crawled out of him, the way it had crawled in — slow, blind, certain, six inches of translucent green skin lit from within by a soft slow pulse — and it did not die, because it was not the kind of thing that dies, and the men who found the body did not understand what they were looking at and so they did the one thing that mattered. They reported it. *Life Form 0 is not inside.* The same words spoken on a dirt road outside Damboa eleven years before, spoken now over a great man's emptied body, and just as true, and just as much the beginning of something rather than the end.

It was Faud who carried it out. Faud, who owed the dead man a debt no living act could pay, who had once asked to be of use to something that was not only taking — Faud took the slug, and the slug held Kaiser, and Faud went where Kaiser had gone, to the rostrum of the world.

---

They called it the Memory Vessel, in the end, as though that had always been Kaiser's title and not the literal description of the thing in the ear. Faud brought it to the United Nations, into the same cold hall where the man it now held had stood and told the nations they were not seeking their permission, and he did not make a speech, because Faud was not a man of speeches and because the point was never the words. The point was that the dead could speak, and that they now included the one man the assembled powers had most wanted silenced.

What happened in that hall is the hinge of the whole eight years, and the survivors never agreed on exactly what was said, because it was not, in the ordinary sense, said. The slug does not work on the living the way it worked through Kaiser; it cannot pour a stranger's memories into a mind that has its own defenses up. And it has its own law, the one Kaiser had learned in a decade of carrying multitudes and that Faud now learned in a single afternoon: the cost of the vessel scales with how wide it is opened. A door cracked to let one fisherman's last minute through is a grief a man can stand inside and walk back out of. A door thrown wide to pour a million of the dead into a single skull is not a thing a body survives whole — Kaiser had opened it that wide only at the very end, and it had ended him. So Faud did not throw it wide. He opened the case, and let the vessel do the one thing it could do even to the guarded and the powerful without unmaking the one who held it — he opened it a careful, terrible inch, and let it amplify, in that room, the simple unbearable fact of being *heard*. And then, one by one, to those who would let it touch them, it offered the dead, a few at a time, no wider than a soul could bear and the bearer survive. Not as accusation. Kaiser had never accused; that was the thing that had made him impossible to fight. It offered a fisherman, a fever-girl, a market woman who died alone. It offered the near-to-half who had died in the fifth year while the species spent its time on something else. And it offered, last, the man himself — the forgiveness he had spent his life unable to lay hands on, the grief over a friend named Justus he had never once set down, the whole patient terrible weight of a man who had carried multitudes and could not carry his own absolution, now carried at last by everyone who would let him in.

You cannot hold a million of the dead in your own marrow, even for a moment, even at a distance, and remain the kind of person who signs the unsigned page. That was what the Memory Vessel did to the United Nations in the fifth year, and it did not undo the killing, and it did not raise the dead, and it did not stop the dying that the Sages were still, that very week, racing to cure too late. It did something smaller and, in the long arithmetic of the eight years, larger. It made the old order, for the length of one impossible session, *feel* the cost of itself. It put the dead in the room where the decisions were made.

---

So the fifth year ended the way it had run, in two minds.

On the one hand, the count that could not be finished. The clerk's spreadsheet too heavy to open. The emptied streets on every continent, the vacated governments, the trucks in the night, the near-to-half. The most powerful nations on Earth revealed to be habits and not laws. The Iron-Eater blight still spreading underneath all of it, eating the metal of a world that no longer had the people to defend it. And a great man dead by the oldest method the powerful keep, for the unforgivable crime of proving that a better order was possible — killed precisely *because* it was working.

On the other hand: it had worked. That was the ember the survivors carried out of the year. Cissy Oliva had wrung a cure out of the dark with borrowed minds and exhausted hands, and though it came too late for most it did not come too late for all, and the all it saved were the seed of whatever came next. Kaiser's Africa had stood when the rich north fell and shown four billion people that the quarry could become a country. The poor had buried the most and carried the most, and in doing so had answered, at the very bottom of the worst year, the oldest question the species asks about itself. And the dead had finally gotten into the room.

And Faud carried Kaiser out of that hall the way he had carried him into it — the case never far from his hand, the slug inside it holding the dead man and the million before him, a debt converted at last into a duty. He did not give it up. Wherever the next year went, the Memory Vessel went with the reformed Akrahuhum killer who had appointed himself its keeper, and so the one thing the assembled powers had most wanted silenced went on existing in the world, portable, patient, waiting for the next room that needed the dead in it.

In the wreckage of it, in the silenced cities and the camps that had held and the wards where the cure had come a day too late and a day in time, the survivors began — quietly, without permission, the way the best things in this world begin — to imagine a new order. Not the African Union Kaiser had dreamed; he was gone, and his dream had been a continent's, and the wound the year had opened was the whole world's. But the *shape* of it was his, and they knew it. A thing that spoke with one voice. A thing with roads to every village and a shield over all of it and a refusal, learned in blood, to ever again spend the time it had on something other than each other. They did not have a name for it yet — that would come the next year, out of the ash, in a borrowed Geneva hall, when the frightened remnant of the world's parliaments sat down together to build the thing and called it, half in hope and half in bitter joke, the United Nations of the New World. But the impulse came first, in the fifth year, in the rubble, before anyone thought to name it: the survivors looked at what the fifth year had killed and what it had taught, and began to try to build the world that should have existed before the dying — the world that, had it existed, might have made the dying smaller, or made it mean something, or made it not at all.

They began, in other words, to do the human thing. They survived the verdict and went on arguing with themselves about what kind of thing they wished to be.

And far out past the radio-loud little world, in the slow cold dark where four militarized civilizations were already on their way, the watching thing that fed on data noted the figure it could not quite finish — near-to-half, and the species still carrying water to each other at the bottom of it — and found, in that single contradictory datum, the most interesting thing it had learned about Earth in five years of looking. Not that they died. That they had spent the worst year of their history proving they were worth the trouble of being saved, and had killed the man who told them so, and had then begun, immediately, to build the thing he died for.

The aliens had three years left to come. Humanity had three years left to decide whether the fifth year had taught it anything at all.

It had not, yet, decided.
