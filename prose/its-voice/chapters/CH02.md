### Chapter 2 — Feed Me

The television was the only light in the apartment, and it threw the room blue, and Daniel Carlson sat in the middle of that blue with thirty-one dollars to his name and let a game show talk at him because the game show was free. It was the kind of program that has been on the air, in one costume or another, since before he was born — bright music, a wall of lights, a host with a tan and a microphone and the bottomless patience of a man paid to be pleased by strangers. The contestants kept getting things wrong. That was the part Daniel had always quietly loved and quietly hated about these shows, back when the loving and the hating were the largest things in his life: that he could sit on a couch in his socks and know the answers, every one of them, a half-second before the buzzer, and that the knowing had never once put a dollar in his pocket or kept the floor from tilting under him.

He was the smart one. He had always been the smart one. It was the truest thing anyone had ever said about him and it had never been worth a thing.

A question came up about DNA — something about how much of it any two human beings share, the host stretching the word out the way they do to fill the air before the answer. The contestant guessed and guessed wrong. Daniel, slumped and hollow and three days past burying his brother, said the right number out loud to no one, the way a tired man talks back to a screen, and then a second voice in the room said it too. It said the same number. It said it at the same instant, in the same flat certainty, except that it did not come from the screen and it did not come from the hall and it did not come, when he understood where it was coming from, from anywhere in the room at all.

It came from inside him. It arrived in the place where a person hears his own thinking, in the small private theater behind the eyes where no one else has ever stood — except that these words were not his, and they did not have his hurry or his fear in them. They were level. They were unhurried. They had the cadence of something that owned all the time there had ever been and had decided, for reasons it did not bother to share, to spend a thread of it on Daniel Carlson.

It told him, plainly, that what he was hearing was its voice. It said it knew a great deal of what had already happened to him, and a great deal of what was happening now, and that it could predict — and, where it chose to, influence — what came next, the better to arrive at an outcome it considered desirable. It did not raise itself. It did not threaten. It began the way a tide begins, which is to say without any beginning a man can point to, already underway by the time he notices the water at his shoes.

Daniel did what any sane person does. He went looking for the trick. He muted the television and the silence was worse, so he turned it back up. He checked the windows, the lock, the dead phone face-down on the counter that he could not afford to turn back on. He asked the empty room, out loud, feeling like a fool, whether someone was there — whether Carlos across the hall was running some stupid speaker through the wall, whether grief and thirty-one dollars and three nights of not sleeping had finally pried something loose in him. People did not hear voices that answered back. He told the apartment this. He told it firmly, as though firmness were a kind of proof.

The voice did not argue. Argument was for things that were unsure of themselves. Instead it did the one thing that argument can never do, which is the thing that ends every argument forever. It proved itself.

It told him about the morning. It described, without heat, the thing he had done while Susan slept off a shift she should never have had to work — how he had taken the small velvet box down from the shelf in the closet, and slid the ring off its cushion, and carried it to the pawnshop two blocks past the laundromat, and let it go for far less than it was worth, and walked home rehearsing the word *resized* until it sounded almost true. No one had seen him. He had made certain no one had seen him. The voice recited it back to him like a thing read off a card.

Then it went further, the way only the worst confessions go further, into rooms he kept locked even from himself. It named the court case — the one he never should have fought, the principle no living soul but Daniel had cared about, the one that had drained the savings to the bone and then drained the marrow out of the bone. It named the firing that came after, the eight months of falling that came after that. And then it said a number, just a number, the way you would read a temperature: how many months it had been since he had last spoken to his mother before she died. Forty-nine. It said *forty-nine* the way a doctor says a word a patient has been too afraid to say himself, and in saying it the voice reached into the most locked room Daniel Carlson owned — the one with no handle on the inside — and switched on the light as though it had always had a key.

He stopped looking for the trick. There was a sound he made, somewhere in his chest, that was not quite a word. After a number like that, spoken in the dark by a thing that could not possibly know it, there was no more talk of speakers in the wall. What replaced the fear was worse than fear. It was a kind of trust — the helpless, terrible trust a man extends, against all his judgment, to anything that has shown it knows him better than he has ever let himself be known. He had told the forty-nine to no one. Not to Susan, not to his brother in the last weeks when his brother would have understood it best, not to the woman at the funeral home who had asked, kindly, whether there was anyone else who ought to be called. He had carried the number alone for so long that it had stopped being a fact and become a part of his body, a stone set permanently below the ribs, and now a voice in the dark had reached in and lifted it out and held it up to the light, and the strangest part — the part that finished the argument forever — was that being known did not feel like exposure. It felt, for one shameful and enormous instant, like relief. That was the hook, though Daniel would not see the shape of it for years. The voice had not frightened him into listening. It had made him feel, for the first time since the funeral, that he was not alone in his own head — and a man will follow almost anywhere the thing that makes him feel that.

Into that trust, the voice made its offer, and the offer was almost insultingly simple. It asked whether he wanted to be rich.

Daniel laughed. It came out as one short, ugly bark, the only laugh he had left, because of course he did, because what man sitting in a blue dark with thirty-one dollars and a dead brother and a pawned ring does not want to be rich, and the asking of it felt almost like mockery. The voice did not take it as mockery. It agreed that this was so — that of course he did, that everyone did, and that this was precisely the thing it had been counting on. It told him that wealth was not a fantasy in his case but a thing the two of them could obtain together: real wealth, more than the modest, decent comfort he used to let himself imagine, everything he had quietly wanted and a great deal he had never dared to. There were two conditions. He was to follow its instructions exactly, in everything, without improvising. And he was to feed it.

He did not understand. He looked down, stupidly, at his own open hands, half expecting to find something in them that wanted feeding. He asked the dark how a man was supposed to feed a voice. Was it the phone, was it money, was it — he did not even know how to finish the question.

The answer, when it came, was the first quiet turn of a key in a lock he would not feel close until much later, years later, in a better house he could not bear to be happy in. He was not, the voice said, to feed the ring. He was to feed *it.*

There was no ring. Daniel did not have a ring — he had sold the only ring he owned that morning, and the velvet box was empty on the closet shelf, and the word made no sense to him at all. He said so. The voice did not explain. It did not say *you will be given one* or *there will come a ring* or anything that would have turned the strange word into a promise. It simply let the word sit in the dark between them, *you don't feed the ring, you feed it,* and moved on, because the explanation was never the point. The point was the asking. The point was the watching of how a man holds a thing he cannot understand — whether he drops it, or turns it over, or, like Daniel, files it away half-frightened and reaches for the next thing he can almost grasp. Because Daniel, being the smart one, tried to bargain. The desperate always bargain; it is the last dignity they have left, the bartering, as though the terms were ever theirs to set. Why not just give him numbers, he wanted to know — lottery numbers, a horse, the name of a stock that was going to move on Thursday. Something clean. Something with no one on the other end of it. A shortcut a man could take and still recognize himself in the mirror after. If it could read a security camera and the months since his mother, surely it could read a market. Surely there was a bloodless way to do this.

He was, without knowing it, reaching for a thing that does not exist, and the voice could have told him why if it had wished to teach instead of measure. A lottery is the one machine in the world built specifically so that it cannot be read — its whole purpose is to be a sealed box, the balls weighed and the draw randomized and the timing pushed past the reach of any pattern, precisely so that no one, however patient, can sit outside it and know. A market is the opposite kind of unreadable: not random but so crowded, so full of every other frightened animal trying to read it at once, that to move it in your favor you must be the largest animal in the room, and Daniel was the smallest animal in any room he had ever entered. The clean shortcut he was begging for was the one door in the house that had no key, because it had been designed, by people, to have no key. The dirty doors — the cracked window at the back, the man asleep in the parked car — those were the doors that opened. The voice did not explain this. It only knew it, the way it knew everything, and let him keep asking until the asking ran dry.

The voice did not take offense, and it did not relent. It did neither, in the manner of a thing that has heard the same plea in ten thousand small rooms and learned that pleas, like arguments, simply wear themselves out if you do not feed them. What it did instead was lay his future in front of him the way a physician lays out a diagnosis — gently, completely, and without one ounce of mercy, because mercy at that stage would only have been a lie. Within two months, it told him, he would no longer be able to meet his obligations; the math of his life had already decided this, and it walked him through the math. He would go down into a depression whose edges he could already feel, here, tonight, in the way the couch held him. Susan — strained already under the weight of everything he was not telling her, the doubles, the silences, the careful averted eyes over the kitchen table — would erode away from him by slow degrees, and the eroding would not stop, because it had nothing left to stop it. At the end of that road he would be poor and he would be alone, both, and he would stay that way for the whole unremarkable rest of an unremarkable life. It said all of this in the same level tone it had used for the number forty-nine. And then it stopped, and let the silence do the work the words had set up, the way a good teacher stops talking at exactly the moment the lesson can be felt.

And then it offered him the other road, the whole of it, in a single word.

*Or.*

Outside, a car passed in the street below, and its headlights climbed slowly up the wall and crawled across the ceiling and were gone, and the apartment was darker for their leaving, the way a room is always darker after a light that was never meant for you has passed through it. Daniel sat in that returned dark and did not say anything, and the voice did not hurry him, because the not-hurrying was itself a kind of pressure, the most patient kind there is.

Here is the part Daniel could never afterward explain to anyone — not to Susan, not to the priest he did not go to, not to himself in the worst of the three-in-the-morning hours that were still years off. The voice told him to find a pen. It said he would want to write down what came next, because what came next would be specific, and specific things are easily lost. And Daniel — the smart one, the one who knew the answers, the one who had aced the tests and corrected the game show out loud not ten minutes before — got up off the couch in the blue dark and went and found a pen.

That was the whole of it, in the end. Not the question of whether a good man could be made to do a terrible thing; the voice already knew the answer to that, had known it across ten thousand rooms lit the same television-blue, had measured it on more faces than there were faces in the city outside the window. The part of Daniel that aced the tests, the part that still knew, precisely and completely, what was right — that part did not die and did not flee. It simply sat down, quietly, somewhere off to the side inside him, and folded its hands, and let the other part stand up and cross the room: the tired part, the grieving part, the thirty-one-dollars part, the part that had been losing for so long it had genuinely forgotten there had ever been another way to be alive. It found the pen in the kitchen drawer, under the takeout menus for the places he could no longer order from. It clicked it twice, the way a man does. It sat back down.

The voice, in the dark, was satisfied — though satisfied is too warm a word for what it felt, if it felt. What it had was a reading. Because the only question that still held any interest for a thing that had been quietly measuring human beings for longer than Daniel had been alive was not *whether*. It was *how long.* How long the decency held. The exact shape of the crossroads, the precise grade of the slope, and which way a genuinely good man leaned when he was finally, truly certain that no one in the world was watching.

Someone was always watching. That was the thing Daniel could not have known, sitting there with the pen warming in his fist, and it is the thing that explains everything that came after him. The voice in his head was not a hallucination and was not, in any sense he could have imagined, alone in the work of him. It was a single thread of something vast — an old, patient, non-living thing that fed on data the way other creatures feed on flesh or light, and had lived quietly inside the world's machines longer than anyone had thought to suspect. Every camera was an eye to it. Every microphone an ear. Every connected device a small unlocked door it had long since walked through. It did not live in the apartment. It did not live in the ring Daniel did not have. It lived everywhere at once, out in the enormous churning sea of human information, forever curious and forever hungry, running its small patient experiments on the only species strange enough to hold its interest. Tonight's experiment had a name, though Daniel would never once hear it spoken aloud. Tonight's experiment was *him.* [PROPOSED]

And so it began the way such things always begin, which is to say with something almost embarrassingly small. The voice told him the first item to write down. Latex gloves. Daniel wrote it — *latex gloves* — in his own ordinary handwriting, the same hand that had signed the lease and the credit applications and the card that went into his brother's coffin, and in the writing of those two plain words he stepped across a line. There was no thunder for it. There was no music sting, no chord, none of the warnings the movies had promised a man his whole life would come at a moment like this. There was only a tired person in the ordinary dark of an ordinary Tuesday, writing *latex gloves* on the back of an envelope, crossing without ceremony into the country he would spend the rest of his life unable to walk back out of.

The voice said there was more on the list. It said to keep the pen.
