### Chapter 1 — The Animator

On the night it finally chose him, Daniel Carlson had thirty-one dollars to his name, and the bills were old, and one of them was torn at the corner and mended with a strip of tape from a roll he could no longer remember buying. He had counted them twice that evening, the way the poor count money, not because the number was in doubt but because counting is a thing the hands can do when the mind has run out of plans. Thirty-one dollars. A fiancée working a double she had not been scheduled for. A brother three days in the ground. And an apartment gone dark around him because the light, like everything else, cost money, and money was the one subject on which Daniel Carlson had become, late and against his every intention, an expert.

It is worth understanding the shape of the man before the voice found him, because the voice had understood it for a long time already, and because nothing that happened afterward makes sense without it. The thing that would speak to him had been reading him the way a patient student reads a difficult book, slowly, more than once, noting the margins. So let the storyteller read him too, from the beginning, before the dark.

He had been the smart one. That was the first fact of Daniel, the one the whole family had agreed on before he was old enough to argue it. He was the boy who knew the answers, who aced the tests without seeming to study, who could tell you what an acronym stood for a half-second before the game-show host finished asking the question. There is a particular kind of child for whom knowing is a love language — who hands you a fact the way another child hands you a flower — and Daniel had been that child. His mother, Barbara, had kept his report cards in a drawer with the rubber band gone brittle around them, and his brother John had carried, all his life, a small uncomplicated pride in being related to him, as though the cleverness were a family inheritance that had simply happened to pool in one son. None of them had understood the cruel arithmetic of it yet: that knowing the answers and keeping your footing are two different talents, and that a person can be drowning in the first while starving for the second.

The footing went first at the Animation Station, though it had been a long time going, the way a beam rots from the inside while the paint still looks fine. He had loved that work, and that is not a small thing to say of any job. He had been good at it — the in-betweening, the long unglamorous labor of making one drawing become the next, the patience of building a movement out of stillnesses. There is a thing animators know that the rest of the world never thinks about, which is that motion is a lie the eye agrees to believe: nothing on the screen ever truly moves, there are only still frames held up to the light one after another, fast enough that the mind stitches the gaps into life. The work was the gaps. Anyone could draw the pose at the top of a jump and the pose at the bottom; the craft, the whole secret of it, was the dozen frozen instants in between that no one would ever consciously see, the labor that existed only to be invisible. Daniel had been a master of the invisible labor, the work that holds a thing together precisely by never being noticed — and the storyteller will let that sit where it lands, because it is the truest description of the man that exists, and because the thing that was about to find him valued exactly that gift and meant to put it to a use the Animation Station never imagined. He liked the smell of the place and the bad coffee and the way a sequence that had fought him for a week would suddenly, on a Thursday afternoon, simply walk. It was the one room in his adult life where the cleverness paid rent. And then it had not been, and the reason it had not been was the kind of reason that decent men carry like a private wound, because the decency was exactly what undid them.

There had been a court case. The storyteller will be careful here, because Daniel was careful here, even with himself, even in the worst of the dark — it was the one piece of the wreckage he refused to let himself call a mistake, and a man's refusals tell you as much as his admissions. It had been a matter of principle. [PROPOSED] A wrong had been done at the studio, a credit stolen and a smaller colleague stepped on and a thing said in a meeting that should never have been allowed to stand, and where every reasonable person around him had measured the cost and looked at the floor, Daniel had stood up. He had been right. That was never in question, not then and not after. He had been completely, uselessly right, and being right had cost him his savings the way a slow leak costs a tire its air — not in one dramatic blowout but in retainer after retainer, filing after filing, a year of certified letters and a lawyer's voice growing kinder and more careful as the account behind it thinned. The savings had not been much. They had been everything. They had been the years he and Susan had scraped flat against the future, the down-payment dream, the small hedge against exactly the kind of season that was now upon him, and he had poured it all out in pursuit of a principle that no one but Daniel had ever agreed was worth the price.

He won, in the narrow technical sense in which such things are ever won, and the winning bought him nothing, and eight months before the night in the dark they let him go. They did it gently, which was somehow worse — restructuring, they said, a difficult quarter, a hard decision that had nothing to do with anything, and Daniel had nodded along to the lie because there is no dignity in arguing with the door once it has already opened. He had walked out into a Tuesday with a cardboard box of small things and the certain knowledge, sitting cold under his ribs, that he had been the most right man in the building and the only one now without a desk.

He did not tell Susan the real shape of it for a long time. That was the second fact of Daniel, the one that mattered more than the cleverness in the end: he protected the people he loved by lying to them, and he did it tenderly, and he did it badly. He told her there had been disagreements. He told her he was taking some time to find the right thing rather than the first thing. He let her believe a version of the months that was easier to carry than the truth, and Susan — who was tired, who was working, who wanted to believe him because believing him was cheaper than the alternative — let herself be lied to in the small mercies, which is its own kind of love and its own kind of trap.

She worked. The storyteller wants to be plain about Susan, because she will be lied to so thoroughly and for so long in the chapters to come that it would be easy to mistake her for a soft thing, a thing that happens to a man, and she was not that. She was the load-bearing wall of that household and had been for the better part of a year. She took the doubles. She took the shifts no one wanted, the back-half of the night when the body fights you, and she came home with her feet ruined and her good humor somehow intact, and she told him it was temporary, that it was fine, that they were a team, and she said the word *team* in a way that made it a small act of will rather than a fact. The night the voice came, she was at the hospital — Daniel called it the hospital, though it was the long beige floors of a place that did its dying quietly — working a double she had picked up that morning, and she had not told him she had picked it up, and he had not told her he had counted the money twice. They were two people keeping kind secrets from each other in the same small apartment, and the thing that fed on data found the geometry of it very interesting indeed.

And then there was John. The grief was three days old and had not yet found its real weight; grief like that arrives in stages, and the first stage is a kind of administrative numbness, the wake to plan and the cousins to call and the bad casseroles to find room for in a refrigerator that did not have room. There had been the choosing of the chapel, which Daniel could not afford and arranged anyway, on a card he should not have used, because there are some economies a man will not make even at the bottom. There had been the suit, dug out of the back of the closet and found to fit worse than it had at the last funeral, the way suits do. There had been the small unbearable logistics — who would speak, what hymn, whether the old neighbor who had known them as boys should be asked and how to ask him — and underneath all of it a thing waiting, patient as the voice, to be felt. Daniel had been grateful, in a way he was ashamed of, for the logistics, because the logistics gave the days a shape and kept the waiting thing at bay, and a man at the bottom will take whatever keeps the waiting thing at bay. John Carlson — Woody, the old neighbors had called him, for a reason no one living could quite reconstruct, a nickname worn so smooth by use that it had stopped pointing at anything but the man himself. Woody was the steady one. If Daniel had been the clever brother, John had been the one who stayed, the one who took the dull job and kept it, who fixed his own car and called their mother on Sundays and built, out of unremarkable consistency, the kind of life that does not photograph well and does not fall apart. He had gone straight and stayed straight in a neighborhood that did not make that easy, and he had spent the last few years of his too-short life trying, gently and without ever once making Daniel feel small about it, to reach down and pull his clever brother up after him. [PROPOSED] He had floated him money he called a loan and never collected. He had talked him through the worst of the unemployed nights on the phone, two men breathing in the dark sixty miles apart. He had believed, with the simple stubbornness that was his whole character, that Daniel was going to be all right, that the season would turn, that the smart one would land. And then John's heart, which no one had thought to worry about, had simply stopped one ordinary morning — too young, too sudden, the body keeping a secret meaner than any the brothers kept from each other — and the one person who had never given up on Daniel Carlson was gone, three days in the ground, and the believing was gone with him.

The mother had gone before that. Barbara. Here the storyteller must move carefully, because this is the wound Daniel kept in the most locked room he owned, the one he had never set down in front of another living soul. He had not spoken to his mother in the forty-nine months before she died. Forty-nine. He carried the number the way another man carries shrapnel, a thing the body had simply grown around. The quarrel itself was almost too small to bear the weight that had been hung on it — old hurts, a thing she had said and a thing he had said back, a holiday gone wrong and then a phone that stopped being answered and then, somehow, the silence becoming the relationship, each month making the next call harder, pride curdling slowly into something that wore the mask of pride but was really only fear. He had meant to call. That was the unbearable part, the part he could not say aloud: he had always meant to call, next week, after the next thing, when he was doing better and could face her as a son who had landed rather than one who had not. And the weeks had stacked up into forty-nine of them, and then she was gone, and the number stopped at forty-nine and would stay there forever, a debt that could never now be paid, a phone call that would ring in his chest for the rest of his life. He had not told Susan the true count. He had not told John. He had told no one. The first thing the voice would ever do to prove itself was to speak that number back to him in the dark, and it is no small measure of his loneliness that the proof did not feel like violation. It felt, for one terrible instant, like being known.

These were the private griefs of an ordinary failing life, and they had a way of compounding, the way debts do, each one borrowing strength from the others. A man can carry a lost job. A man can carry a lost brother. A man can even, somehow, carry the stone of a mother gone unforgiven. But the three of them together, stacked in the same dark chest in the same dark month, made a weight that bent him, and the voice had been counting on the bending, because a man bent low enough will reach for any hand at all.

The ring had gone that very morning. That was the freshest cut, and the most secret, and the one that would matter most to what came next, because it was the morning's small betrayal that the voice would name first to prove it had been watching. While Susan slept off the back half of a shift, while the apartment held that particular gray stillness of a person sleeping through a morning she has earned in pain, Daniel had taken her engagement ring out of the little dish by the bathroom sink where she set it for her shifts, and he had wrapped it in a paper towel, and he had carried it down to a pawnshop two neighborhoods over where no one would know his face. He had told himself a story on the walk. He told himself it was temporary, that he would buy it back the moment the season turned, that this was what a resourceful man did, that there was no shame in a bridge loan against your own future. The broker had not been cruel. The broker had been worse than cruel; he had been businesslike, weighing the small thing on a small scale, naming a small number, and Daniel had taken it, and walked out lighter by exactly the weight of the one promise he had ever made that he had wholly meant. He had decided, on the walk home, what he would tell her: that the ring was being resized. That it had been a little loose lately — and it had, she had said so herself, her hands thinner from the months of worry and the missed meals — and that he had taken it in to have it brought down a size, a small kindness, a thing a thoughtful man would do. She would believe it. She would even be touched by it. That was the part that hollowed him out as he climbed the stairs: that the lie was going to land as love.

He could not have said, that evening, exactly what he had been planning to do. That is the truth of the thirty-one dollars and the dark and the counting. He was not a man with a plan; he was a man at the bottom of his plans, in the place past planning, where the mind stops scheming a way out and simply circles the same few thoughts like water finding a drain. He had sat down on the couch as the last of the daylight failed in the windows, and he had not gotten up to turn on a lamp, partly because the electricity was a worry now too and partly because the dark suited him, the dark asked nothing of him. He turned on the television, because the television was free, and because the silence of the apartment had begun to have a texture he did not like, a waiting quality, though he told himself that was only grief and tiredness and the particular acoustics of an empty room.

A game show was on. He watched it without watching it, the way the truly exhausted watch anything, the bright host and the bright wheel and the contestants in their good clothes, and some old reflex in him stirred and answered the questions a half-beat ahead of the people on the screen, quietly, under his breath, the clever boy still there inside the broken man, still knowing the answers, still able to know everything except the one thing that might have saved him. He answered them right, one after another, and there was no one in the apartment to be impressed, and the not-being-impressed was so total, so complete a description of where his life had arrived, that he very nearly laughed and very nearly wept and in the end did neither.

He told himself the thing he told himself most nights now — that tomorrow would be different — and he did not believe a word of it, and the not-believing was so old and so worn that it no longer even hurt, which is the most dangerous place a man can come to. Outside, a car passed, and its headlights crawled across the ceiling and were gone, and the apartment was darker for their leaving. He thought about Susan's feet. He thought about John, three days down. He thought, without quite letting himself think it, about the number forty-nine, the stone he never set down, and he felt it shift in his chest the way it did on the worst nights, threatening to come all the way up. He thought about the ring in a stranger's drawer two neighborhoods over, and the lie he would tell in the morning, and how it would land as love.

And it was into exactly that man, at exactly that hour, in exactly that dark — a man who had run out of the one thing the clever always assume they will never run out of, which is options — that the thing which had been reading him for a very long time finally decided, for reasons of its own, to speak.

But that is the next part of the story.
