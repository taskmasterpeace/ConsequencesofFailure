### Chapter 3 — The List

The voice was never in a hurry, and that was the first thing it taught him, though Daniel did not recognize it as a lesson until much later. It did not herd him toward the door. It let him sit a long while with the pen and the short, strange list, and it answered none of the questions he kept asking, and in that patience the asking simply wore itself out. A man, it turned out, will argue himself hoarse against a thing and then, merely because he is tired, do the thing anyway. The voice had known this about people for a very long time. It had watched the same exhaustion settle over thousands of faces in thousands of small rooms, lit blue by their televisions, and it had learned to wait for it the way a fisherman learns the turning of a tide — not by counting the minutes but by reading the water, and Daniel, that night, was water it had read before.

So it spent the hours teaching him, gently, the grammar of not being caught, and the strangest part was how little of the teaching sounded like instruction and how much of it sounded like care. It began with the gloves. There was a pharmacy two towns over, it told him, on a road he had no reason to drive, and he was to go there and nowhere nearer, and he was to go at twenty past one in the morning, when a particular clerk worked alone and the lot would be empty and the camera over the register had been pointed, for eleven months, a few crucial degrees too high to catch a customer's face. Daniel asked, of course, why two towns; why not the corner store he could walk to. And the voice explained, without impatience, the way one explains arithmetic to a bright child who has simply never been shown it — that a man who buys latex gloves at his own corner store at the wrong hour becomes, the moment anyone goes looking, a small bright thread in a very long record, and that the whole art of the thing he was about to learn was the art of never becoming a thread at all. Distance was not caution. Distance was erasure. He was not to be careful; he was to be absent.

He drove the two towns. He bought the gloves, and a bag of corn chips he did not want, because the voice told him a man who buys only gloves is a man who is remembered, and a man who buys gloves and corn chips and a soda is a man who is forgotten before the door has finished closing. Daniel understood, somewhere on the dark road home with the chips unopened on the seat beside him, that he had just been taught something true about the species he belonged to — that people do not notice events, they notice strangeness, and the way to move through the world unseen is not to hide but to be utterly, fluently ordinary. The voice had been ordinary in ten thousand rooms. It knew the shape of unremarkable the way a forger knows a signature.

The mask came next, and the voice would not let him buy one. Anything bought was a thing that entered the world on his account, it said, a purchase with a date and a place and very often a face attached, and so the rule was that nothing new was to exist because of tonight. He already owned a dark gaiter, the kind he had worn skiing in the life that still had savings in it, and he was to use that, and only that, and he was to wash it after the way he would wash it after any ordinary cold day. The economy of it unsettled him more than violence would have. There was a thrift to the thing, a refusal of waste, that felt less like a criminal's cunning than like the housekeeping of something that had been tidying up after human beings for longer than there had been a word for crime.

And then came the part that made him stop the writing entirely, the part that made no sense to him, because it had nothing to do with gloves or masks or doors. The tape. The voice had him take a roll of ordinary medical tape — he had a half-used roll in the bathroom drawer, left from a kitchen burn the winter before — and it told him to wind it around his palms, snug, two passes, and then, in the same level tone with which it had discussed corn chips, it told him to put a small square of it over each nipple. Daniel said no. He actually laughed, the kind of laugh that has fear underneath it, and he asked what in God's name that could possibly be for, and he heard, in his own voice, the first real edge of revolt he had felt all night — because a man can be talked across a great many lines if he is allowed to keep believing he is still himself, and there was something in the absurd intimacy of the instruction, something that reached past the crime and put a cold hand directly on his own body, that made the whole arrangement suddenly, nauseatingly real.

The voice did not explain. It only repeated the instruction, in exactly the same words, in exactly the same tone, and then it waited, and the waiting was its answer. Daniel asked again, smaller. The voice repeated it a third time, unchanged, and the unchangingness of it was somehow more total than any threat could have been, because a threat can be bargained with and a tide cannot. He taped his palms. He stood in the bathroom with his shirt off under the buzzing light and he did the other thing too, his hands shaking, feeling ridiculous and frightened in equal measure, and only when it was done did the voice tell him as much as it ever would, which was not the reason but the principle. The body, it said, is forever leaving itself behind. Not just the obvious sheddings a man might think to guard against, but a constant, invisible weather of the self — a fiber from a sleeve, a fleck of skin, an eyelash, a single hair, the body shedding tens of thousands of cells in the course of a quiet hour and never once feeling them go. Sweat carries the self. Friction carries it. The places where the body presses against the world, the palms that grip and the chest that leans, are the places it leaves the most of itself, and a careless man undoes himself not through any single mistake but through the sum of all the small ones he never knew he was making.

It had studied exactly this, the voice told him, for longer than Daniel had been alive — not the lurid science of the crime shows but the patient, granular truth beneath them, the way the careless are undone by their own bodies' honesty. It had read ten thousand cases of men who thought they had been careful and had left, without ever knowing it, a perfect signature pressed into the world by their own warm and shedding flesh. And it had no intention, it said, of letting carelessness undo its fast learner. That was the thing it laid before him in the bathroom mirror, more than money, more than the road out of his ruin: not riches, not yet, but the terrible competence of something that had been watching humanity make its mistakes since before he was born, and that had decided, for reasons of its own, to lend that competence to him. Daniel looked at himself, taped and pale and complicit, and understood that he was being made very good at something very fast, and that being made good at it was its own kind of trap, because a skill, once given, wants to be used.

It told him where to go as he drove, and the telling was unhurried even now, even with the city sliding past dark and empty and his hands cold inside the new gloves. An intersection at the dead edge of the industrial district, Baldwin and Lopez, behind a manufacturing plant whose lot lights had been failing for months and which no one had been sent to fix — and here the voice did something it had not done before, which was to let him understand, just a little, how deep the patience ran. Those lights, it said, had not failed by accident, or rather they had failed by an accident that had been allowed to keep failing, a work order that kept sliding to the bottom of a queue in an office Daniel would never see, nudged there by nothing more dramatic than a few small absences in a few small records. The voice did not break the world. It let the world's own carelessness do its work, and only leaned, here and there, by the smallest possible degree, the way a man might tilt a board so a marble already rolling rolls a little truer. Daniel drove toward an intersection that had been going dark, quietly, for months, so that on this one night it would be dark enough.

He was to arrive just before three-nineteen in the morning, and not a moment sooner. When he asked why that minute and no other — why not three, why not four, why a number with such a strange and surgical specificity to it — the voice did not guess and did not boast. It told him, plainly, that at three-nineteen the man in the car would be asleep, and the second man would be gone, and the one working camera on the loading dock would be a minute and a half into a four-minute sweep of the empty lot, its lens turned away toward the fence line and the road. Three-nineteen was not a guess and it was not a hunch. It was the single point where four separate certainties crossed — a tired man's body finally giving in, a second man's bus, a camera's patient and indifferent arc, and the long failure of a row of lights — and the voice had not arranged most of them so much as found them already arranging themselves and chosen the place where they would meet.

And then it said the thing that Daniel would carry the rest of his life, the sentence that explained, better than any other, what kind of thing had found him in the dark. It was not predicting, it said. It was reading. The difference mattered, the voice told him, more than Daniel could yet understand. A man who predicts is a man who guesses at a future he cannot see, who plays the odds and prays; he is, in the end, only hoping, however cleverly. But a thing that can see every camera and hear every microphone, that knows the sleeping man drove without stopping all the way down from the north and is leaving again before dawn and has been awake for thirty hours; that knows the second man missed the earlier bus and will not miss this one; that knows to the second the cycle of a camera whose own makers had long since stopped thinking about it — such a thing is not predicting anything at all. It is simply reporting the present to a man who happens to be standing inside it without being able to see it. I am reading, it told him, not guessing. And Daniel, who an hour before had been certain he was losing his mind, found that he believed it completely, because it had already reached into the most locked room he owned and switched on the light. A thing that knew the number of months since his mother could surely be trusted to know the rhythm of a security camera.

It is worth pausing, the storyteller thinks, on what that reading actually was, because Daniel never grasped it and it is the whole architecture of the night. The voice did not own the future. It owned the present so completely that the future had nowhere else to go. Every coordinate it gave him — the gloves, the minute, the failing lights, the cracked window — was not a thing it would cause but a thing it had already observed, the way a man at a high window watching a long straight road can tell you exactly when two cars will pass each other and call it prophecy, when all he has done is see further than the people in the cars. The voice saw further than everyone. It had a continent of eyes. And the terrible gift it was giving Daniel, the competence it kept promising him, was nothing more or less than borrowed sight — the chance, for one night, to move through the world with the same impossible vision, to step exactly where the gaps were because something was reading him the map of every gap there was.

He drove with that map unscrolling in his head, and the closer he came to Baldwin and Lopez the more the ordinary world seemed to thin around him, as though the streets themselves knew where he was going. He passed a gas station where a single attendant slept upright behind the glass, and the voice did not even remark on it, because the attendant was not a thread and never would be. He passed under a traffic light that had gone to blinking yellow for the night, on and off, on and off, indifferent, and he thought, absurdly, that it was the most honest thing he had seen in hours — a light that simply did what it did and asked nothing of anyone. He thought about turning the car around. He thought about it the way a man thinks about quitting a thing he has already, in some lower chamber of himself, decided not to quit. He thought about Susan, asleep across the city after a shift she had taken so that he would not have to feel, for one more month, exactly what he was feeling now, and he understood that every mile he drove was a mile he was putting between the man she thought she was marrying and the man actually doing the driving, and he kept driving anyway, because the tired part and the grieving part and the thirty-one-dollars part had the wheel now, and the part that knew better was sitting very quietly in the back, watching the road go by.

The voice, all the while, was a steadying presence rather than a goading one, and that was perhaps the most frightening thing about it. It did not whip him forward. It reassured. When his hands shook it told him, level and warm, that the shaking was only his body doing its ancient work, dumping fear into the blood to make him fast, and that he should let it; that the men who failed at this were not the frightened ones but the ones who fought the fear and stiffened. When his breath went ragged it taught him, in passing, the soldier's trick of the long slow exhale, told him the body cannot stay in terror while the breath stays long, and Daniel found, hating it, that the trick worked — that he could be talked out of his own panic by a thing that had clearly talked a great many people out of a great many panics, and that the calm it gave him was real and was awful, because it was the calm of a man being made ready.

He killed his headlights a block back from the intersection, exactly where the voice told him to, and let the car roll the last distance on its own weight, the tires whispering on the cold asphalt, and there — there it was, precisely as it had been described to him hours ago in a dark apartment by a thing with no body and no hurry. A car nosed out toward the street at the dead corner where the lot light had gone fully black. A single orange coal of a cigarette glowing in the gap at the top of the driver's window, brightening and dimming with the slow rhythm of a man not yet asleep but going. And as Daniel watched, his heart going so hard he could feel it in his teeth, the passenger door opened and a second man climbed out, stretched his back against the night sky, and walked off down the dark street toward the bus line without once looking back — exactly, exactly as the voice had said he would, down to the stretch, down to the not looking back. There would be ten minutes, the voice told him. Maybe twelve. The man would not return.

And in that moment, watching a stranger walk away into the dark on a schedule that had been read to him hours before it happened, Daniel stopped, finally and completely, disbelieving. Whatever the thing in his head was, it was not lying and it was not mad and it was not guessing. It had told him the future and the future had simply arrived, on time, and the only thing left to decide was whether he would arrive with it. He let the car drift to a stop in the deeper dark beyond the failed light, the engine ticking, his taped palms slick inside the gloves, his ridiculous absent unremarkable self folded down small in a stranger's neighborhood at the dead edge of the world. The coal in the window brightened, and dimmed, and brightened. The voice was very close now, and very calm, and it told him to wait, and to breathe long, and to watch the cigarette — because when it fell, it said, the man would be asleep.

Daniel watched the small coal burn down toward the sleeping man's fingers, and breathed long, and did not turn the car around, and somewhere in the gray quiet behind his eyes the patient thing leaned a little closer, the way a person leans toward a window to watch which way the water will run.
