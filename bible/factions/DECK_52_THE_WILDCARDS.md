## What a Wildcard is (and isn't)

A **Joker** — Deck 52 calls them **Wildcards** in the field and on paper — is a former operative who **volunteered** for the Wildcard Initiative and came back changed. No suit. No rank on the ladder. A card that can stand in for any other card, which is exactly why the deck is afraid of it.

These are **not** Living Super Weapons. The Greys did not touch them. There is no gift here, no chosen-one selection, no clean alien physics. A Wildcard is a **human being remade by other human beings** — by surgery, by RoboForge-adjacent nanostructure, by chemistry and implantation and a consent form they signed in a room they were not allowed to record. The augmentations are crude where the LSW powers are elegant. They **leak**. They **wear out**. They **charge rent.** Every Wildcard is a person who traded something they will never get back for something they were promised would be worth it, and the central horror of the program is how often the trade was honest and the person took it anyway.

> **Tone lock (per `prose/deck-52/THE_DOOR_the-burned-woman.md`):** grounded, prestige, haunting. Never cape-and-tights. A Wildcard's power should read like a medical event that learned to walk, not like a superpower. The cost is always legible on the body and in the room.

The Initiative lives in the legal gray that Deck 52's international reach creates: the work is done in jurisdictions that have not written laws against it yet, on volunteers who waived the right to be protected from it, for a company that is, on paper, somewhere else. It is the most contested thing inside the company — see **The Wildcard Question** at the end.

## WILDCARD 01 — "TALLY" / **Idris Achebe-Lund** *(the sympathetic one)*

> A man who can no longer be lied to, and who would give anything to be lied to again.

**Before.** Idris Achebe-Lund `[PROPOSED]`, forty-one, a forensic accountant out of Lagos by way of Oslo — a half-Nigerian, half-Norwegian man with a quiet marriage, a daughter named Sefi, and a gift for finding the one wrong number in ten thousand right ones. He was never a soldier. Deck 52 used him as a **Diamonds** contractor, an auditor who untangled the money trails of the people the company was paid to study. He was good and he was boring and he was happy, which is a combination the Initiative does not usually get to work with.

**Why he volunteered.** Sefi was eight when the seizures started, and the only protocol that touched them was a Deck 52 medical asset Hearts ran out of a clinic in Geneva — the kind of care that does not have a price because it is not for sale to people like Idris. Naomi Pierce's office made the offer the way the company always makes it: not a bribe, a *door*. One door led to his daughter on the protocol for life. The other door led back to Lagos and the seizures. He has said, the one time he was asked, that he did not volunteer out of desperation. He volunteered out of arithmetic. He found the one right number in his own life and he paid it.

**What they did to him.** The Initiative built him into a **living polygraph**, and then past it. A lattice of nanostructure threaded through the cranial nerves and the visual cortex reads the body of whoever he is looking at — micro-flush, pupil, the breath you take before the lie, the heat that blooms under the jaw — and renders it to him not as data but as **sensation**. When a person in front of him says something untrue, Idris **tastes it.** Lies have flavors. A small social lie is faintly metallic, almost pleasant, like biting foil. A practiced, structural lie — the kind a man tells when his whole life is the cover — coats the back of his throat with something he describes only as *the taste of a coin held too long in a warm hand.* He has never once been wrong. The company has tested him against its own best liars and he has never once been wrong.

**The hard limit / the rent.** He cannot turn it off, and it does not distinguish kindness from deceit. *Every* untruth lands — the waiter who says the kitchen's still open, the doctor who says it won't hurt, his wife on the phone saying she's fine. He has lost the entire register of human mercy that runs on small, loving lies. Worse: the lattice is **degrading the tissue it reads through.** Each year his color vision narrows; he now sees the world in the washed sepia of an old photograph, and Hearts' projections give him perhaps six years before the optic structures fail entirely and he goes blind — a blind man who can still taste every lie told in a dark room. The Initiative knew this when they built him. He signed the part of the form that said so.

**The sensory quirk you won't forget.** He keeps **hard candy** in every pocket — lemon drops, the sour kind — and he is sucking one in almost every scene. It is the only thing that overwrites the taste of a lie fast enough to let him keep his face still. A man crunches a lemon drop in a negotiation and across the table someone's stomach drops, because the Diamonds all know what it means when Tally reaches into his pocket.

**How Deck 52 deploys him.** Negotiations, interrogations, internal counter-treachery, and — the use that is quietly killing him — **loyalty screening of Initiative volunteers**, which means Idris is the man who sits across from the next desperate parent and tastes whether they mean it. He is the company's most trusted instrument and its guiltiest one.

**Need and fear.** Deck 52 needs him because in a company built on a betrayal between two friends, a man who cannot be lied to is worth more than any weapon. They fear him for exactly the same reason. He has had dinner with Charles Sapphire **once.** Charles, who never raises his voice and never lies, is the only person Idris has ever sat across from and tasted *nothing* — and neither man has ever told anyone what that silence meant to the other.

**The story waiting to happen.** Sefi is fourteen now. She has started lying to him the way teenagers do — small, ordinary, necessary lies — and her father can taste every one, and he has never let her see his face change, because the alternative is telling her what he is, and he would rather go blind first. He may get to do both.

## WILDCARD 02 — "PALLET" / **Yelena "Lena" Marsh** *(the in-between)*

> A woman who can put any pain to sleep, including the kind that was keeping someone alive.

**Before.** Yelena Marsh `[PROPOSED]`, called Lena, thirty-six, a combat-trauma nurse — British Army, then two tours contracting for Deck 52's **Hearts** suit running forward aid stations in places the news did not cover. She was the one who held the hand. She was the one who, when there was no morphine left and a nineteen-year-old was coming apart on a table, learned to talk a body down from agony with nothing but her voice and her thumbs pressed into the right places. She believed, genuinely, in Total Operative Welfare. She believed she was the merciful part of the machine.

**Why she volunteered.** Zealotry, dressed as compassion. After the aid station at — `[PROPOSED location TBD]` — where she ran out of everything and listened to four men die over eleven hours, Lena stopped sleeping and started *recruiting herself.* When the Initiative came to her she did not need to be persuaded; she needed to be **chosen.** She told Dr. Lena Cho (Queen of Spades) — and the shared first name is a coincidence the company finds either funny or sinister depending on the day — that she wanted to be the thing she had needed and not had. She wanted to be able to *reach in* and stop pain. Cho's program gave her exactly that, which is the trap, because the Initiative is most dangerous when it gives a person precisely what they asked for.

**What they did to her.** A subdermal nanostructure mesh across both hands and forearms — RoboForge-adjacent, grown rather than implanted — that interfaces with the peripheral nervous system of whatever living thing she is touching skin-to-skin. With contact and intent, **Lena can edit sensation.** She can switch off pain the way you'd mute a channel. She can hold a screaming, dying man in total, blissful silence while the medics work. She can also, and the company knows this, hold a man in something that is not bliss. Her hands run a few degrees too warm now, always, and when she's working they glow faintly under the skin — not light, exactly, more like the dull orange you see through your own fingers held over a flashlight.

**The hard limit / the rent.** Three things. First: **it only works skin to skin**, close, intimate — she has to be holding you, which means every use of her gift looks like an act of love and several of them have not been. Second: **the nervous system keeps a ledger.** Pain she switches off does not vanish; it *defers*, and it comes due. A man she carries through an injury without agony will, hours or days later, be hit with the entire bill at once — and the company has learned to use this, to keep an asset functional through a mission and let him collapse after extraction, which Lena calls, with a flat voice, *the layaway.* Third, and worst: **prolonged contact lets her edit more than pain.** Fear. Grief. The will to resist. She is not supposed to be able to do this. She can. She did it once, to a prisoner, for sixteen hours, on Cho's instruction, and the man told them everything and then could not feel anything ever again, and Lena has not been the same since.

**The sensory quirk you won't forget.** She **wears gloves indoors** — thin black surgical gloves, always, even at dinner — and she takes them off only to work or to be alone, so that the act of a person removing a single glove in a room has become, around Deck 52, the most frightening gesture in the company's vocabulary. People go quiet when Pallet ungloves a hand.

**How Deck 52 deploys her.** Officially: casualty extraction, the most humane medic alive, the public face of why the Initiative might be defensible. Unofficially: she is **Clubs' and Spades' favorite interrogation asset**, the one who leaves no marks because she leaves nothing at all, and the gap between the official and unofficial Lena is the exact width of the Wildcard Question.

**Need and fear.** Hearts (Dr. Mara Vance) wants her decommissioned and protected — Vance sees in Lena the proof of her entire argument, a healer turned into an instrument of refined cruelty. Spades (Cho) sees the most precise tool the program has ever produced and will not give her up. Lena herself does not know which of them is right, and she is the only Wildcard who has asked, out loud, to be turned off.

**The story waiting to happen.** Lena is starting to feel, faintly, the deferred pain of *other people* — the layaway is leaking backward through the mesh into her own nervous system. Every man she ever spared, she is slowly beginning to pay for. The merciful part of the machine is becoming its conscience the hard way: one borrowed agony at a time.

## WILDCARD 03 — "PRESSER" / **Tomas [surname redacted] / "the Quiet Man"** *(the frightening one)*

> The room goes silent when he wants it to. So does everything in it.

**Before.** This is the one the company does not have a clean file on, and that is by design. The man the records call **Tomas** `[PROPOSED — surname redacted even in-world]` was, before, a **Clubs** breacher and demolitions specialist out of the former Yugoslavia — a flat, polite, unremarkable professional who was very good at the part of the job that involves silence and closed doors. Colleagues from before remember almost nothing about him, which they now find significant. He had no family the company could find. He had no debts. He had, as far as anyone can establish, no reason to volunteer at all, which is the part that frightens the people who knew him: he is the only Wildcard who seems to have done it **out of curiosity.**

**Why he volunteered.** Nobody is sure, and he has never said, and Idris Achebe-Lund — Tally — was brought in to screen him and reported, white-faced, that he could not get a reading, because the man never said anything that was either true or false. He answered questions with questions. He told the intake board he wanted to know *what it was like on the other side of fear*, and then he smiled, and the board approved him anyway, because Spades wanted to test the most invasive build the Initiative had ever attempted and here was a volunteer with nothing to lose and no one to lose it to.

**What they did to him.** The Initiative built Tomas into a **field of silence.** An implant suite seeded through the brainstem and the deep nuclei lets him project, within roughly a ten-meter radius, a **suppression field** — a forced damping of the central nervous system of everyone inside it. Inside Presser's field, voices drop to whispers because lungs won't fully drive the breath. Trigger fingers slow. Adrenaline won't fire. Panic, the thing that keeps people alive in an ambush, simply does not arrive. He turns a room of armed, frightened, capable people into a room of calm, sluggish, *compliant* people who cannot understand why they are not more afraid. He walks into the worst moment of a firefight and the firefight gets *quiet*, and then it gets over.

**The hard limit / the rent.** The field has no targeting. **It includes him, and it includes everyone he loves, and it includes the hostage.** He cannot exempt a single nervous system in range, which means he can never use his gift to *save* a specific person — only to flatten an entire room, friend and enemy and bystander into the same gray calm, and let the people with the clearest objective walk out of it. And the deeper cost: the field is **bleeding upward through his own suppression.** To run it he has had to be progressively numbed himself, and the build has flattened his affect year over year until he now lives, permanently, inside a milder version of what he does to others. He does not feel fear. He has stopped feeling most things. He is becoming the room he creates. The company's psychologists believe he volunteered, at some level, *to get here* — that the curiosity was about whether a person could put down the entire weight of being alive and keep walking. The answer appears to be yes, and it appears to be the most frightening thing the Initiative has ever made, because it is the one Wildcard who is **content.**

**The sensory quirk you won't forget.** When his field is active, **all the small ambient sound of a space dies at once** — the HVAC hum, the buzz of a light, the white noise you never notice until it's gone — so that the first sign Presser has entered a room is not a sound but the *absence* of one. People describe it as the feeling of a plane's engines cutting out. And he is always, faintly, **humming** — one tuneless low note, the only sound he seems able to still make for himself, the only thing inside his own dead-calm field that is his.

**How Deck 52 deploys him.** Hostage rooms. Mutinies. Standoffs the company needs to end without a body count and without a witness who can describe what happened, because nobody inside the field remembers it clearly. He is the company's instrument for the situations that are too delicate for force and too dangerous for negotiation. He is also, increasingly, the instrument they send when an **Initiative volunteer changes their mind** — and that is the use that no one inside Deck 52 will say out loud, including the people who order it.

**Need and fear.** Deck 52 needs Presser because he is the only asset that can defuse the very crises the company's other Wildcards might cause — he is the off-switch for the room. They fear him because he is the off-switch for *any* room, including theirs, and because he is the proof of what the Initiative produces when it succeeds completely: not a monster, not a hero, but a calm and curious man who has been edited all the way down to a hum, and does not appear to mind, and could walk into the boardroom on the fifty-second floor and make everyone in it stop being afraid of him at the exact moment they should be most afraid.

**The story waiting to happen.** Charles Sapphire has never met him. Charles has read the file and declined the meeting three times, and the people around him have noticed that the Dealer — who faces everything — will not be in a room with the one asset of his own company whose gift is to take away a man's fear. The day those two are finally in the same room is a story by itself.

## THE WILDCARD QUESTION

The Initiative is the only thing inside Deck 52 that the senior command has never been able to settle, and the fight has names.

**Dr. Mara Vance — Queen of Hearts — opposes it.** Hearts is the suit of Total Operative Welfare, and Vance argues, with the moral floor the suit was built on, that you cannot run a welfare mandate and a human-experimentation program inside the same company without one of them being a lie — and she knows which one she thinks it is. Her case is not squeamish; she has seen the field results. Her case is precise: **consent obtained from the desperate is not consent**, the company manufactures the desperation it then asks people to escape (a sick daughter, a debt, a war that broke them), and a Wildcard is therefore not a volunteer but a person the company cornered and called brave. She wants the Initiative frozen, the existing Wildcards protected and treated as patients rather than assets, and Idris, Lena, and Tomas pulled off the missions that are visibly killing them. She is losing. She knows she is losing. She keeps the fight on the record anyway, because Hearts' whole reason to exist is to be the part of the deck that says *no* — and because she suspects she is the only person in the company Charles is actually listening to.

**Dr. Lena Cho — Queen of Spades — and Naomi Pierce — Queen of Diamonds — push it.** Cho's argument is the engineer's: the Initiative is the literal expression of the Transhuman Augmentation pillar, the things the world is about to face do not care about Earth's bioethics committees, and a company that will not make the fittest will be erased by something that will. *Not the survival of the fittest — the creation of the fittest.* She regards Vance's position as a luxury that the burned woman on the *Carl Vinson* already disproved. Pierce's argument is the strategist's, and colder for being kinder: she does not love the program, she loves the **edge**, and she points out — accurately — that every Wildcard volunteered, that the doors were real, that Idris's daughter is alive *because* of the trade, and that a company that refuses to let desperate people make hard bargains is not moral, it is merely comfortable. Pierce is the one who wrote the consent framework. She is also the one who lies awake about it, which Idris could tell you, because he tasted it once and said nothing.

**What Charles privately believes.** This is the part nobody in the company knows, and the part that matters most.

Charles Sapphire is a religious man who built a war machine out of grief, and the Wildcard Initiative sits on the exact fault line of that contradiction. He believes — genuinely, in the part of him that prays before things — that what the Initiative does is a **sin**, that you do not have the right to remake the image a person was made in, that every Wildcard is a soul he will personally answer for, and he means *answer* the way only the devout mean it. And he has never shut it down.

He has not shut it down because he did the math once, the same way he did it after Emily, following a chain all the way to its end: the Alliance of Four is coming, his own intelligence says so, the burned woman says so, and on the day they arrive the question will not be whether the Initiative was righteous. The question will be whether he had the fittest things humanity could make standing between the armada and a sidewalk where someone's Emily is walking with a garment bag over her shoulder. He believes he is committing a sin to prevent a worse one. He believes he is damning himself on purpose, with his eyes open, the way he believes Vaughn damned himself by accident and called it cleverness — and the distinction, that he *knows* and chooses anyway, is the only thing that lets him sleep, and it is also the thing Mara Vance is most afraid of, because a man who has decided to be damned for a good reason cannot be argued out of it. He can only be the kind of man who keeps the photograph on the desk, and visits the chapel, and signs the part of the form that says *I know what this costs*, and keeps the Initiative running anyway, one ruined and necessary soul at a time.

> *Deck 52's motto is "Death is like a deck of cards." The Wildcards are the cards Charles dealt himself — and he is the only player at the table who already knows the house always wins.*
